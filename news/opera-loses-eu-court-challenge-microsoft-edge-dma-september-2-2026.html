Opera loses EU court challenge over Microsoft Edge DMA exemption

The European Union's General Court has upheld the Commission's decision not to designate Microsoft Edge as a gatekeeper service under the Digital Markets Act, rejecting Opera's argument that regulators misapplied the law's criteria.

By VISIONE Wire · September 2, 2026 · 11:57 CEST

Opera has lost its attempt to overturn the European Commission's decision to keep Microsoft Edge outside the Digital Markets Act's list of designated gatekeeper services. On September 2, the General Court of the European Union upheld the Commission's 2024 decision, concluding that regulators were entitled to accept Microsoft's evidence that Edge was not an important gateway between businesses and end users.

The ruling matters beyond a dispute between two browser makers. It tests how much discretion the Commission has when a service crosses the DMA's numerical thresholds but argues that its real market role does not justify gatekeeper treatment. The court's answer, in this case, was that the Commission stayed within the law when it accepted Microsoft's rebuttal.

What is confirmed: The General Court rejected Opera Norway's challenge in case T-357/24. Edge met the DMA's quantitative thresholds, but the Commission concluded in 2024 that the browser did not qualify as an important gateway. The court upheld that conclusion on September 2, 2026. Microsoft remains a designated DMA gatekeeper for other services, but Edge itself remains outside the designated list.

Why Edge was outside the DMA in the first place

The Digital Markets Act creates special obligations for large platform services that function as important gateways between businesses and users. The framework uses quantitative thresholds to identify services that are likely to qualify, but companies can submit rebuttal arguments explaining why a particular service should not be designated despite meeting those thresholds.

Microsoft did exactly that after notifying the Commission in 2023 that several services met the numerical criteria. The Commission opened market investigations into Edge, Bing and Microsoft Advertising, along with Apple's iMessage. In February 2024, it closed those investigations without designating any of the four services.

The Commission's explanation was not that Microsoft itself was too small for the DMA. Microsoft had already been designated as a gatekeeper for other core platform services. Instead, the finding was service-specific: the Commission said Edge did not operate as an important gateway through which business users reached end users.

That distinction is central to the DMA. Gatekeeper status applies to specific core platform services, not automatically to every product offered by a company that has already been designated elsewhere.

Opera challenged the Commission's reasoning

Opera Norway brought the case before the EU's General Court, arguing that the Commission had misapplied the DMA criteria when it accepted Microsoft's rebuttal. Opera's commercial interest is straightforward. Browser defaults, distribution and operating-system integration can influence how users reach the web, and Opera competes directly with Edge in Europe and elsewhere.

The General Court did not accept Opera's challenge. Reuters reported that the judges found no error in the Commission's decision to accept Microsoft's sufficiently substantiated arguments that Edge did not constitute an important gateway.

The judgment therefore leaves the Commission's 2024 decision intact. Edge is not subject, as a designated core platform service, to the full package of gatekeeper obligations that apply to services on the DMA list.

What gatekeeper designation would have meant

The DMA is designed to make digital markets more contestable and fairer by imposing obligations on designated gatekeepers. Depending on the service, those obligations can affect self-preferencing, interoperability, data use, default settings, access conditions and the ability of business users to reach customers outside a gatekeeper's ecosystem.

Designation is not a general declaration that a company has violated competition law. It is a regulatory status that brings a service within a specific set of ex ante obligations. That is why the Edge case turns on whether the browser is an important gateway, rather than on whether Microsoft has broad market power in software or operating systems.

The European Commission says 23 core platform services are currently designated under the DMA. Microsoft remains one of the companies designated as a gatekeeper, alongside Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta and Booking, but the list is built service by service.

The ruling gives the Commission room to weigh market reality

The most important legal implication is that meeting the DMA's quantitative thresholds does not automatically end the analysis. The law allows rebuttals, and the General Court has now backed the Commission's ability to accept one when the underlying evidence is sufficiently substantiated.

That does not mean every company crossing the thresholds can escape designation with a persuasive filing. The Commission can reject rebuttals, open deeper market investigations and continue monitoring a service after deciding not to designate it. The 2024 Edge decision itself explicitly said regulators would keep watching market developments and could revisit the question if conditions changed materially.

The inference is that future DMA disputes may focus increasingly on evidence about how a service actually connects businesses and consumers, not only on headline user counts or corporate size. Today's judgment strengthens the Commission's position when it argues that the statute requires both quantitative screening and qualitative market assessment.

A long-running browser competition fight

The Opera-Microsoft rivalry has a much older European regulatory history. Opera filed a competition complaint against Microsoft in 2007 over browser practices. That broader sequence ultimately contributed to regulatory action around browser choice in Windows, and the European Commission fined Microsoft €561 million in 2013 for failing to comply with commitments to offer users a browser choice screen.

The current DMA dispute is legally different. It is not a repeat of the older antitrust case and does not decide whether Windows browser integration is lawful in every context. Instead, it asks whether Edge itself should be designated as a core platform service under the newer DMA framework.

That difference is easy to blur because both disputes involve browser competition. The confirmed outcome today is narrower: Opera failed to overturn the Commission's non-designation decision for Edge.

What does not change after the judgment

The ruling does not remove Microsoft from the DMA. Microsoft remains designated in relation to other covered services. It also does not prevent the Commission from investigating Microsoft under other EU competition or digital rules, nor does it permanently immunize Edge from future designation if its market role changes.

Likewise, the judgment should not be read as a finding that Edge lacks competitive advantages from its integration with Windows. The question before the court was whether the Commission lawfully concluded that Edge was not an important gateway within the DMA's statutory test.

What comes next

The immediate effect is continuity. Edge remains outside the designated DMA service list, and the Commission's 2024 market-investigation decision stands. Opera can assess the judgment and any available appeal route, but no successful appeal has been announced at the time of publication.

For regulators, the decision adds judicial support to a DMA enforcement model that combines hard thresholds with case-by-case assessment. For browser competitors, it means the route to changing Edge's status is more likely to depend on new market evidence or a successful higher-court challenge than on reopening the same arguments rejected by the General Court.

The broader regulatory contest around browsers is far from over. The Commission has separately scrutinized browser choice and default-setting practices under other DMA designations, particularly in relation to mobile operating systems. But on the specific question before the court on September 2, the line is clear: Edge met the numbers, Microsoft rebutted the presumption, the Commission accepted that rebuttal, and the General Court upheld the Commission.
