Prime Video commits more than $2 billion to Latin America through 2030

Amazon is making one of its clearest long-term bets on Latin American entertainment, with more local originals, major live-sports rights and a wider Prime Video marketplace.

By VISIONE Wire · August 21, 2026 · 18:00 CEST

Prime Video plans to invest more than $2 billion in Latin America between 2027 and 2030, a multiyear expansion that puts Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Colombia and Chile at the center of Amazon's next streaming push.

The commitment covers original productions, licensed programming and live sports. Reuters reported the announcement on August 20, while Amazon executives presenting the strategy in Mexico City described the region's current momentum in local talent, stories and audiences as a reason to scale investment rather than treat Latin America as a secondary market.

The headline number is large, but the more important story is how Amazon intends to spend it. Prime Video says it will more than double the number of local originals produced across the five core countries compared with 2026 levels. More than 25 new local titles are planned for 2027 alone, with another wave expected after that.

What Prime Video has announced

The investment runs from 2027 through 2030 and is spread across five of the region's biggest entertainment markets: Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Colombia and Chile. Prime Video's 2027 slate is expected to include new seasons of La Oficina, Tremembé and Menem, alongside Marília and other scripted series, films and unscripted formats.

That expansion matters because locally produced streaming hits increasingly travel beyond their country of origin. For global platforms, regional originals are no longer only a tool for customer acquisition in one territory. A successful Brazilian, Mexican or Argentine title can also become catalog value for audiences elsewhere, especially as dubbing, subtitles and algorithmic recommendations reduce distribution friction.

Prime Video's strategy is therefore both local and export-oriented. Amazon is funding productions that can strengthen subscriptions and engagement inside Latin America while also looking for the next title capable of escaping its home market.

Brazil is one of the central markets

Brazil is a major piece of the plan. It is one of the five countries named in the investment strategy and already plays a prominent role in Prime Video's sports offering. Under Amazon's 11-year global NBA agreement, Prime Video now carries more than 200 NBA games per season in Brazil and Mexico.

The company says its NBA coverage has also expanded into Argentina, Colombia and Chile. Sports are especially important because they solve a problem that scripted streaming does not: live programming creates appointment viewing. A drama can be watched days or weeks later. A playoff game loses much of its value once the result is known.

For Prime Video, that means sports can increase repeat visits, reduce churn and give the service a reason to be opened every week rather than only when a major series arrives.

Mexico gets an additional football bet

Prime Video is also becoming a new home for the Mexico national football team. The company says it will carry 38 home matches over four years, beginning next month.

That gives Amazon another high-frequency sports property with strong national identity and commercial potential. It also reinforces a broader streaming-industry trend: platforms are increasingly willing to compete for sports rights that once belonged almost exclusively to traditional television networks.

The logic is straightforward. Sports deliver large audiences at a fixed time, create advertising inventory and can convert casual viewers into long-term subscribers. But rights are expensive, and profitability depends on whether those viewers stay for the wider service.

Prime Video is becoming more than a streaming library

The Latin America plan also expands Prime Video's role as a storefront. Later in 2026, customers in Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, Guatemala, Paraguay and Peru are expected to gain the ability to subscribe to third-party streaming services directly through the Prime Video app.

Amazon also plans to introduce rentals and purchases of premium entertainment in those markets, with Argentina gaining the same transactional option. That model pushes Prime Video beyond a conventional subscription service and toward an aggregation platform where customers can watch Amazon originals, live sports, partner channels and individually purchased titles in one interface.

For Amazon, aggregation has strategic value. Even when viewers are paying for content from another provider, Prime Video can remain the place where the transaction and viewing session begin.

Why Latin America is attracting more streaming capital

Amazon is not alone in increasing regional spending. Netflix and other global platforms have invested heavily in Latin American production, building studios, commissioning local-language series and turning regionally rooted stories into international releases.

The attraction is a combination of audience scale, creative talent and the possibility of producing globally competitive content at a different cost structure from Hollywood. Latin America also contains several large, culturally distinct markets rather than a single homogeneous audience, which creates room for both regional blockbusters and highly local programming.

For streaming companies, the challenge is balancing expansion with economics. The industry has moved past the era when subscriber growth alone justified almost unlimited spending. Investors now focus much more closely on margins, advertising revenue, retention and the financial return on expensive sports and production deals.

That makes Amazon's four-year commitment notable. It is not simply adding a handful of shows. The company is signaling that Latin America will remain part of its capital allocation strategy even as streaming businesses are under pressure to prove that scale can translate into durable profit.

What is confirmed and what is still unclear

The investment amount, the 2027-2030 period, the five core markets and the expansion of originals and sports have been publicly announced. What remains less clear is how the more than $2 billion will be divided among countries, productions, licensing, sports rights, marketing and technical expansion.

Amazon has also not provided a title-by-title budget breakdown. That means it is too early to say which country will receive the largest share or whether one segment, such as sports, will absorb a disproportionate part of the total.

The company has, however, provided a measurable content target: more than doubling local original output from the 2026 baseline, with over 25 new titles planned for 2027. That gives the market a benchmark against which execution can be judged.

What happens next

The first visible test will arrive quickly. Prime Video's new Mexico national-team coverage begins next month, while the 2027 slate will show how aggressively the company converts the investment announcement into released programming.

For viewers in Brazil and the wider region, the practical effect should be a larger flow of locally produced series, films and reality formats alongside a heavier sports calendar. For creators and production companies, the commitment means more commissioning opportunities and more competition among global platforms for regional talent.

The bigger question is whether Amazon can turn those investments into sustained engagement rather than temporary spikes around a hit show or major game. That will determine whether the $2 billion-plus commitment becomes a durable expansion of Prime Video's Latin American business or simply another costly chapter in the streaming wars.
