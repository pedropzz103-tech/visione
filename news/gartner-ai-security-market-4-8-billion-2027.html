AI security market to reach $4.8 billion in 2027, Gartner forecasts

Spending on tools built specifically to secure artificial intelligence is expected to surge as companies deploy more autonomous systems and confront prompt injection, access-control weaknesses and software-supply-chain risk.

By VISIONE Wire · August 26, 2026 · 12:02 CEST

The market for products designed specifically to secure artificial intelligence is on track to expand from $2.835 billion in 2026 to $4.783 billion in 2027, a 68.7% increase, according to a new Gartner forecast published August 26. The research firm expects spending to approach $7.7 billion in 2028, turning AI security from a specialized niche into a rapidly growing category inside the broader cybersecurity market.

The forecast reflects a basic shift in enterprise risk. Companies are no longer only trying to stop employees from leaking sensitive data into public chatbots. They are deploying AI systems that can call tools, retrieve internal information, make recommendations, write code and, increasingly, take actions with limited human supervision. Those capabilities create attack surfaces that conventional application security products were not designed to understand on their own.

Gartner's figures are forecasts rather than audited market results, and they should be read that way. But the direction is consistent with another Gartner finding released a day earlier: in a survey of 316 senior executives and risk managers, AI-enabled discovery of cyber vulnerabilities ranked as the most critical emerging risk in the second quarter of 2026.

Four categories are becoming a new security stack

Gartner divides the securing-AI market into four main areas: AI application security, AI usage control, AI governance platforms and AI gateways. The distinction matters because the security problem spans both the AI model itself and the people, applications and data connected to it.

AI application security is forecast to remain the largest named category in 2027, reaching $851 million from $508 million in 2026, a 67.5% rise. AI usage control is expected to grow from $433 million to $749 million, the fastest percentage increase among the four major categories at 73%.

AI governance platforms are forecast to climb from $275 million to $462 million, up 68%. AI gateways, which can sit between enterprise applications and AI services to enforce policy, inspect requests and control access, are expected to rise from $251 million to $429 million, a 70.9% increase. Gartner groups another $2.292 billion of 2027 spending under other securing-AI products and services.

Combined, those categories imply that enterprises are building a security layer around AI rather than relying on a single product. A company may need governance to define which models can be used, a gateway to enforce those rules, application-security tools to test AI systems and usage controls to watch how employees and agents interact with them.

Prompt injection and access control are central risks

One of Gartner's most consequential projections is that more than half of successful cyberattacks on AI agents by 2029 will exploit access-control weaknesses and prompt injections. That is a forecast, not a measured present-day incident rate, but it captures the architecture problem created by autonomous systems.

Prompt injection attempts to manipulate an AI system through instructions embedded in user input, documents, websites or retrieved data. A simple chatbot may respond with an incorrect answer. An agent connected to email, cloud storage, code repositories or financial systems can create a much larger consequence if the injected instruction changes what tools it calls or what information it reveals.

Access control is the second half of the problem. Even a model that follows instructions perfectly can become dangerous if it has broader permissions than the task requires. Security therefore depends not only on model safeguards but also on identity, authentication, authorization, logging and the principle of least privilege around every tool the AI can reach.

Why traditional security is not enough by itself

Traditional cybersecurity products remain essential. Firewalls, identity systems, endpoint protection, software composition analysis and application testing do not become obsolete because AI is added to a workflow. The issue is that they often see the AI application as ordinary software and may not understand model-specific behavior.

Gartner analyst Shailendra Upadhyay said traditional tools require significant updates to address AI-specific threats and pointed to specialized AI usage-control and application-security products as fast-growing areas. The same release highlights third-party and open-source software in AI projects as a supply-chain concern.

That supply-chain angle is particularly important because modern AI systems are assembled from many components: foundation models, open-source libraries, vector databases, orchestration frameworks, plugins, connectors, APIs and datasets. A vulnerability or malicious component in any layer can affect the system even if the underlying model is secure.

Agentic AI changes the economics of cyber defense

The rapid growth forecast is also tied to agentic AI. An agent that merely summarizes information is relatively contained. An agent that can execute code, update customer records, move files, approve workflows or interact with other agents creates both operational value and a larger blast radius when something goes wrong.

This is why spending growth in AI security can outpace growth in AI software itself for periods of time. Enterprises that move from experiments to production discover that reliability, auditability and control are prerequisites for scaling deployment. Security becomes part of the cost of turning a model demo into infrastructure.

Gartner's August 25 emerging-risk report makes a similar point from another direction. It warns that AI can make vulnerability discovery faster and more accessible, potentially allowing weaknesses to be found and weaponized more quickly than traditional remediation processes can respond. The firm recommends faster patching, automated remediation, stronger third-party controls and updated risk assumptions.

The forecast also points to consolidation

The money flowing into the sector is likely to reshape the vendor landscape. Gartner expects established companies to remain strong in governance platforms and AI gateways because those products can extend existing data, API and enterprise-governance systems. AI application security and usage control, by contrast, are attracting more startups.

That mix creates a familiar cybersecurity pattern: a fragmented first wave of specialists followed by acquisitions as larger security vendors buy capabilities they do not want to build from scratch. Gartner explicitly expects increased consolidation and M&A as the market expands.

For customers, consolidation can simplify procurement but can also blur product categories. Buyers will need to distinguish tools that genuinely inspect AI-specific behavior from conventional security products relabeled around AI.

What is confirmed and what is still uncertain

Confirmed: Gartner published the forecast on August 26 and estimates 2026 spending on securing AI at $2.835 billion, rising to $4.783 billion in 2027. The firm forecasts nearly $7.7 billion in 2028. It also published segment-level estimates for application security, usage control, governance platforms and gateways.

Forecast: the 68.7% growth rate, the 2028 market size and the expectation that more than half of successful attacks on AI agents will exploit access-control weaknesses and prompt injection by 2029 are forward-looking Gartner projections, not measurements of future outcomes.

Uncertain: the category boundaries are still evolving. Security vendors are adding AI controls to existing platforms while startups create specialized products, so some spending may shift between categories as the market matures.

What businesses should watch next

The practical question is whether AI security becomes a standalone budget or is absorbed into broader cloud, identity, application-security and governance spending. The answer will shape which vendors win the market and how quickly specialized startups are acquired.

The second question is regulation. As governments demand more documentation, risk management and accountability around high-impact AI systems, governance and monitoring tools may be purchased not only for cyber defense but also for compliance.

Finally, the rise of autonomous agents will test whether security architectures can keep permissions narrow while still allowing useful automation. That tension is likely to define the next phase of enterprise AI: the systems become more valuable precisely when they gain the ability to act, and that same ability raises the cost of a mistake or compromise.

Bottom line

AI security is becoming a market in its own right. Gartner's forecast suggests organizations will spend billions not just on deploying artificial intelligence but on controlling what it can access, how it behaves and how attackers may manipulate it. The projected jump to $4.8 billion in 2027 is therefore less a story about a new software category than about a new operating reality: once AI moves from answering questions to taking actions, cybersecurity has to move with it.
