OpenAI says Jalapeño chip beats leading AI accelerators on speed and efficiency

The first measured results for OpenAI's custom inference chip put hard numbers behind its attempt to control more of the AI stack, from models and software down to silicon.

By VISIONE Wire · August 25, 2026 · 18:01 CEST

OpenAI has published the first measured benchmark results for Jalapeño, its first custom AI inference chip, claiming substantial gains in both energy efficiency and response speed against leading commercial accelerator systems. The figures were released on August 25 and cover three public model families: GPT-OSS 120B, DeepSeek R1 670B and Kimi K2.5 1T.

The results are significant because they move Jalapeño from a strategic chip announcement into a measurable infrastructure project. OpenAI and Broadcom unveiled the processor in June, describing it as a purpose-built accelerator for large-language-model inference. At the time, the company said early tests suggested better performance per watt than current systems. The new publication provides the first detailed numbers behind that claim.

There is an important caveat. The benchmark report is produced by OpenAI, and the company selected the tested workloads, operating points and comparison systems. The numbers are therefore best treated as vendor-reported performance, not as an independent certification of superiority across every AI workload.

What OpenAI measured

OpenAI says it tested Jalapeño using InferenceX, a public benchmark from SemiAnalysis designed to evaluate the full process of serving AI requests rather than isolated chip arithmetic. The company says it compared systems at matched user experience, looking at useful AI work per unit of power while also measuring latency.

On GPT-OSS 120B, OpenAI reports roughly 1.9 times higher peak mixed throughput per kilowatt than the comparison system and about 1.7 times lower end-to-end latency. Minimum time between tokens was reported at roughly 2.7 times lower, while throughput at the comparison system's previous best token-latency point was dramatically higher.

For DeepSeek R1 670B, OpenAI reports about 1.7 times higher peak mixed throughput per kilowatt and 3.6 times lower end-to-end latency. On Kimi K2.5 1T, the largest public model in the test, OpenAI says Jalapeño delivered about 1.5 times higher peak performance per watt and 3.4 times lower end-to-end latency.

Across all three model families, the company summarizes the results as roughly 1.5 to 1.9 times more AI work per watt at peak throughput and 1.7 to 3.6 times lower end-to-end latency than the systems used for comparison. For highly interactive workloads, OpenAI says performance was 2.1 to 4.1 times higher.

Power is becoming part of the AI product battle

The emphasis on performance per kilowatt is not accidental. AI companies increasingly face a physical constraint alongside the familiar race for model quality: electricity, cooling, data-center capacity and accelerator supply all determine how much intelligence can actually be served to users.

OpenAI says Jalapeño is rated at 700 watts, although measured sustained power remained at or below 550 watts on the workloads tested. The company argues that measuring useful work per unit of power is more meaningful than simply comparing performance per chip because the infrastructure required to power and cool accelerators is becoming a major cost driver.

That matters particularly for inference, the repeated computation performed every time a model answers a prompt, completes a coding task or takes a step inside an agentic workflow. Training attracts much of the attention around AI supercomputers, but inference becomes an ongoing operating expense once products reach millions of users.

A chip designed around language models

OpenAI describes Jalapeño as a blank-sheet architecture designed specifically for modern and future language-model inference. The company says the chip, memory, networking, serving software and rack-scale system were co-designed to reduce data movement and communication delays.

That design targets a basic split inside LLM inference. The prefill stage, when a system processes the input prompt, is compute-heavy. The decode stage, when it generates tokens, depends more heavily on memory bandwidth. Communication between chips can create additional latency. OpenAI says Jalapeño attempts to keep more model state local and coordinate compute, memory and networking as one system.

The company also says AI itself accelerated the chip's development. Jalapeño moved from initial design to tapeout in nine months, according to OpenAI, with its models helping explore implementations, optimize arithmetic circuits and shorten verification loops. OpenAI says Codex with GPT-Astra later helped bring three open-weight model families to high performance within two months.

Why this matters for Nvidia, Broadcom and the wider chip market

Jalapeño does not mean OpenAI is abandoning Nvidia or other hardware suppliers. OpenAI explicitly says it expects to continue deploying Nvidia accelerators and hardware from other partners for both training and inference. The strategic change is that OpenAI now has a first-party option it can optimize around its own workloads.

That gives the company another lever in negotiations over cost, supply and performance. CFO Sarah Friar described the strategy on August 25 as maintaining a portfolio across Microsoft, Nvidia, AWS, AMD, Broadcom, Cerebras, CoreWeave, Oracle, SB Energy and SoftBank while building directly where tighter co-design creates leverage.

Broadcom remains central to Jalapeño. The June launch identified Broadcom as the silicon implementation and networking partner, with Celestica supporting boards, racks and system integration. The project therefore illustrates a broader shift in AI infrastructure: leading model companies are increasingly designing custom accelerators without necessarily becoming fully vertically integrated semiconductor manufacturers.

What is confirmed and what is not

Confirmed by OpenAI: Jalapeño is working first-party silicon, has been tested on three public model families, and is planned for deployment inside OpenAI's compute infrastructure by the end of 2026. The company says a second generation is deep in development and a third generation is already taking shape.

Vendor claim, not independent validation: the reported performance advantage over commercial systems comes from OpenAI's own benchmark publication. Independent labs and customers will need to reproduce the results across more models, batch sizes, latency targets and real production workloads before broad conclusions can be drawn.

Still unknown: OpenAI has not published a complete cost-per-chip figure, manufacturing yield, total rack cost or a public price for external buyers. It also has not said that Jalapeño will be sold as a standalone commercial accelerator.

What comes next

The most important milestone is production deployment. OpenAI says it plans to begin putting Jalapeño into its own infrastructure by the end of the year while continuing production qualification, software maturation and validation across more models. That transition will show whether benchmark advantages survive at data-center scale.

If they do, the payoff is larger than faster chatbot responses. More efficient inference can lower the cost of running coding agents, research systems, enterprise assistants and high-volume APIs, while reducing the power required per completed task. For a company spending heavily on compute, even incremental gains can compound across enormous volumes.

The broader industry question is whether custom silicon becomes a durable advantage for frontier AI companies. Google already operates TPUs, Amazon has Trainium and Inferentia, and other hyperscalers are expanding their own accelerator programs. OpenAI's Jalapeño adds another heavyweight to that trend and increases pressure on the traditional GPU-centered model of AI infrastructure.

Bottom line

OpenAI's August 25 results give Jalapeño its first detailed performance story: higher throughput per watt, lower latency and a deployment target before the end of 2026. The figures strengthen the case that OpenAI is becoming a full-stack infrastructure company, not only a model developer.

But the strongest claims still come from OpenAI itself. The next test is independent replication and, more importantly, production economics at scale. If the reported gains hold up, Jalapeño could become one of the most consequential pieces of OpenAI's strategy to reduce inference cost and loosen its dependence on any single accelerator supplier.
