Alibaba launches HK$80 billion share placement to fund full-stack AI push

The Chinese technology group says every dollar of net proceeds from the roughly US$10.2 billion Hong Kong offering will go into artificial intelligence, adding fresh capital to one of the world’s largest corporate AI investment programs.

By VISIONE Wire · August 23, 2026 · 18:00 CEST

Alibaba Group is seeking HK$80 billion, about US$10.2 billion, through a new share placement in Hong Kong and says it intends to direct 100% of the net proceeds into its artificial-intelligence strategy. The company announced the proposed transaction on August 23, describing the financing as part of an effort to extend its position in global AI and expand what it calls its full-stack capabilities.

The transaction is notable not only for its size but also for the clarity of its stated use of funds. Alibaba said the proceeds are intended for AI capabilities including infrastructure expansion and enhancement. Reuters reported that the broader spending plan covers chips, infrastructure and the development and deployment of AI models.

According to a term sheet reviewed by Reuters, Alibaba plans to place 710 million ordinary shares at HK$112.70 each, a discount of about 3.6% to the previous close. Reuters said the deal would be the largest primary follow-on offering ever by a Hong Kong-listed company and the third-largest primary follow-on share sale globally in 2026, behind offerings from Alphabet and Intel.

A capital raise built specifically around AI

Alibaba’s announcement is unusually direct: the company says the net proceeds are not being raised for general corporate purposes, acquisitions or balance-sheet repair. They are earmarked for the company’s AI stack.

That stack reaches from the physical layer to the software layer. Alibaba operates a major cloud business, develops its own Qwen family of AI models and has been investing in semiconductor and computing capacity as it tries to reduce infrastructure bottlenecks and compete with both Chinese and U.S. technology groups.

The placement therefore turns a strategic ambition into a financing event. Instead of funding the next stage of expansion solely from operating cash flow, Alibaba is asking equity investors to help finance the buildout directly. Existing shareholders face dilution, but the company gains a large pool of capital without adding conventional debt.

The offering is being made to certain non-U.S. persons outside the United States under Regulation S. Alibaba said the placement shares have not been and will not be registered under the U.S. Securities Act, meaning the transaction is structured as an offshore offering rather than a U.S. public sale.

The timing follows a sharp rise in AI spending

The fundraising arrives only days after Alibaba reported quarterly results showing how expensive its AI expansion has already become. For the April-to-June quarter, revenue rose 9% while net profit fell 75% year over year. Capital expenditure increased 75% to 67.68 billion yuan, or roughly US$10 billion, as the company spent heavily on chips and computing infrastructure.

At the same time, the part of the business most closely tied to that investment is growing rapidly. Alibaba’s cloud and AI-services revenue rose 45% to 48.44 billion yuan during the quarter, according to Reuters. That gives management a clear argument for continuing to spend: infrastructure costs are rising dramatically, but the cloud and AI businesses they support are also expanding much faster than the company as a whole.

Alibaba has already committed to invest 380 billion yuan, approximately US$56 billion, in AI and cloud infrastructure over a three-year period. Reuters reported that the company had already deployed about half of that planned amount by the end of June.

Chief executive Eddie Wu has argued that the spending should generate returns over time. Reuters reported that Alibaba expects its AI capital expenditure to reach break-even within roughly three years, with proprietary chips and improving infrastructure economics potentially helping margins.

Why the deal matters beyond Alibaba

The placement is another sign that the AI race is becoming a capital-markets story as much as a software story. Training and serving advanced models requires data centers, accelerators, networking, memory, power and cooling at enormous scale. Companies that want to remain at the frontier increasingly need financing strategies measured in tens of billions of dollars.

That pattern is visible across the industry. U.S. hyperscalers have sharply increased capital spending, Nvidia has partnered with major financial institutions to establish platforms intended to mobilize hundreds of billions of dollars for AI infrastructure, and semiconductor companies are signing long-term supply agreements for advanced memory and packaging.

Alibaba’s move adds a major Chinese counterpart to that trend. The company is effectively telling investors that AI is not a side project funded from excess cash. It is a core capital-allocation priority important enough to justify one of the largest equity offerings of the year.

It also highlights the different constraints facing Chinese technology companies. U.S.-China technology tensions and export controls can limit access to certain advanced chips, making domestic semiconductor development, infrastructure efficiency and supply diversification strategically important. Alibaba’s full-stack approach is partly about performance, but it is also about controlling more of the technology chain.

What is confirmed and what remains open

Confirmed by Alibaba: the company has proposed a HK$80 billion placement of newly issued ordinary shares; the transaction targets non-U.S. investors in offshore transactions; and 100% of net proceeds are intended for full-stack AI capabilities, including expansion and enhancement of AI infrastructure.

Reported by Reuters: the deal involves 710 million shares priced at HK$112.70 each, represents a discount of about 3.6%, and has attracted strong investor interest. Reuters also described the transaction as the largest primary follow-on offering by a Hong Kong-listed company.

Still uncertain: the final allocation of the capital among chips, data centers, model development and deployment; the exact schedule for deploying the new funds; and the ultimate return on the expanded investment program. The placement remains subject to market and other conditions.

The next test is execution

Raising capital is only the first half of the equation. Alibaba now has to prove that additional AI infrastructure can translate into sustained cloud growth, stronger model adoption and eventually higher earnings.

The company’s latest quarter shows both sides of that bet. AI-linked revenue is rising quickly, yet the cost of competing at the frontier is already weighing heavily on profit and cash generation. The new placement gives Alibaba more room to keep building without slowing the program, but it also raises the bar for future returns.

If the company can convert infrastructure spending into durable cloud demand and a stronger Qwen ecosystem, the HK$80 billion raise could look like strategic ammunition. If AI monetization fails to keep pace with capital intensity, investors will instead focus on dilution and the widening gap between revenue growth and near-term profitability.

For now, the signal is unmistakable: Alibaba is choosing expansion over restraint and is willing to tap equity markets at historic scale to finance the next phase of its AI race.
