Amazon ties up to $60 billion of AI-chip spending to Qualcomm

The agreement is bigger than a supplier win. It gives AWS another custom-silicon path for inference while giving Qualcomm a credible route from mobile chips into hyperscale AI infrastructure.

By Pedro · September 9, 2026 · 09:39 CEST

Amazon has entered a multi-generation AI data-center collaboration with Qualcomm that can tie as much as $60 billion of commercial activity to Qualcomm server chips, systems, technology and manufacturing services.

Qualcomm announced the technical collaboration on September 8, saying the companies will work on customized silicon for large-scale AI inference and on optical connectivity up to 1.6 terabits per second. The public press release did not disclose the commercial ceiling.

The economic structure appears in Qualcomm's SEC filing. Qualcomm issued an Amazon affiliate a warrant for up to 25 million Qualcomm shares at an exercise price of $161.26. The warrant vests in tranches tied to commercial agreements, binding purchase orders and actual purchases, up to a maximum of $60 billion in payments. The filing says 3.75 million shares vested at issuance based on initial purchase commitments.

Reuters independently reported the arrangement and described it as part of Qualcomm's attempt to expand beyond smartphones into AI data centers. Reuters also reported that Qualcomm is targeting $15 billion in annual data-center chip revenue by 2029.

What is confirmed: Qualcomm and Amazon are collaborating across multiple generations of custom AI-inference silicon and data-center connectivity. Qualcomm's SEC filing ties warrant vesting to as much as $60 billion of Amazon payments and sets the warrant at up to 25 million shares with a $161.26 exercise price. The $60 billion figure is a ceiling tied to milestones, not a guarantee that Amazon will spend the full amount.

VISIONE analysis: this is a hyperscaler diversification deal, not just a Qualcomm sales contract

AWS already designs its own Trainium and Inferentia accelerators, while the wider AI market remains heavily dependent on Nvidia. The Qualcomm agreement adds another route: customized silicon co-developed around Amazon's infrastructure needs rather than a purely off-the-shelf accelerator purchase.

That matters because hyperscalers increasingly compete on the economics of inference. Training grabs headlines, but serving models continuously turns power efficiency, memory bandwidth, networking and chip utilization into operating-cost questions. Qualcomm is explicitly pitching its data-center roadmap around performance per watt and token throughput, which aligns with that pressure.

The optical part of the agreement is also important. Qualcomm says the collaboration extends to 1.6T connectivity and future generations. At rack and cluster scale, accelerator performance is constrained not only by the chip itself but by how quickly data moves among processors, memory and network fabric. The deal therefore spans compute and interconnect rather than treating them as separate purchases.

The $60 billion ceiling is four times Qualcomm's 2029 annual data-center target

Qualcomm has said it wants to reach $15 billion in annual data-center revenue by 2029. The Amazon-linked commercial ceiling is $60 billion.

Simple arithmetic puts the maximum value of the Amazon-linked payments at four times Qualcomm's stated 2029 annual data-center revenue target. That does not mean Amazon will contribute four years of Qualcomm data-center revenue, because the filing does not disclose the payment schedule and the full $60 billion may never be reached. It does show the potential scale of this single customer relationship relative to Qualcomm's own ambitions.

The warrant itself also illustrates the incentive structure. At the stated $161.26 exercise price, 25 million shares represent about $4.03 billion of exercise value if fully vested and exercised for cash. That is not the warrant's fair value, and cashless exercise is permitted, but it gives a transparent sense of the equity exposure attached to the commercial milestones.

A chronology of Qualcomm's data-center push

October 2025: Qualcomm introduced the AI200 and AI250 rack-scale inference platforms, marking a renewed push into server-class AI acceleration.

June 24, 2026: Qualcomm expanded the roadmap with its Dragonfly C1000 CPU, HBC memory technology and AI300 accelerator, and said it had multi-year, multi-generation agreements with leading data-center customers. The same day it announced an agreement to acquire Modular, adding an AI software layer intended to work across heterogeneous hardware.

September 8, 2026: Qualcomm disclosed the Amazon collaboration publicly. Its SEC filing revealed the warrant and the $60 billion maximum payment milestone.

This sequence matters because Qualcomm is not entering the market with a single accelerator. It is assembling CPU, inference acceleration, memory architecture, networking, custom silicon and software. The Amazon deal is the strongest evidence so far that a hyperscaler is willing to connect that stack to a long-duration commercial framework.

What Amazon gets

Amazon gains another source of specialized AI infrastructure and more bargaining power across its supplier base. A custom-silicon partnership can be tuned to AWS workloads and data-center constraints in ways that standard merchant silicon cannot always match.

The warrant structure also aligns Amazon's economics with Qualcomm's success. As purchase milestones are reached, Amazon can gain more equity exposure. Similar structures have appeared in other large cloud-supplier relationships because they partially recycle the upside of a customer's own spending back to the customer.

For AWS customers, however, the practical impact is not yet clear. Qualcomm and Amazon have not announced specific EC2 instance types, launch dates, pricing, benchmark results or software compatibility details. Until those appear, this remains infrastructure strategy rather than a product customers can evaluate directly.

What Qualcomm gets

Qualcomm gets something unusually valuable for a new data-center entrant: a hyperscale design partner plus a pathway to volume. The company's smartphone business remains large, but its long-term growth case increasingly depends on extending its low-power compute expertise into PCs, automotive, edge systems and data centers.

Amazon can help validate that move. Large cloud operators impose demanding requirements around reliability, deployment automation, thermal design, networking, software support and supply continuity. Passing those gates is materially different from announcing a benchmark or reference platform.

The risk is concentration. If a meaningful share of Qualcomm's data-center ramp becomes tied to Amazon, delays or architecture changes at AWS could have an outsized effect on Qualcomm's targets. The public filing does not provide enough timing detail to quantify that exposure.

What remains uncertain

The companies have not disclosed which parts of the future silicon are Amazon-specific, who owns the resulting intellectual property, which fabrication process or foundry will be used, or when the first production systems will deploy.

The $60 billion figure is explicitly a maximum tied to a decade-long warrant that expires in September 2036. It should not be treated as committed revenue. Only 3.75 million of the 25 million warrant shares vested at issuance, which indicates that most of the economic upside remains contingent on future milestones.

There are also no public performance numbers comparing the future Qualcomm-Amazon silicon with Nvidia GPUs, AWS Trainium or other custom accelerators. The competitive consequence will depend on delivered cost per token, software maturity, utilization and availability, not the headline value of the agreement.

Bottom line

The Qualcomm-Amazon agreement is strategically important because it shows AI infrastructure continuing to fragment beyond a single dominant merchant-chip model. AWS is adding another custom-silicon relationship, while Qualcomm is converting years of low-power compute and connectivity expertise into a serious bid for hyperscale inference.

The most useful number is not the stock-market reaction. It is the structure: up to $60 billion of payments tied to a 25 million-share warrant, against Qualcomm's own target of $15 billion in annual data-center revenue by 2029. If even a substantial fraction of the ceiling materializes, Amazon will become central to Qualcomm's data-center story. If it does not, the gap between headline potential and realized volume will be equally informative.
