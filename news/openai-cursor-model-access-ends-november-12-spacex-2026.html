OpenAI plans to cut off Cursor model access on November 12 after SpaceX takeover

The AI company says it is winding down a nearly four-year supply relationship after Cursor changed hands, but the transition does not mean OpenAI models disappear from the editor entirely.

By VISIONE Wire · Published August 29, 2026 · 12:01 CEST

OpenAI says it intends to stop supplying its models to Cursor through the companies' existing commercial agreement, proposing November 12, 2026 as the shutoff date after Cursor was acquired by SpaceX. The decision turns a major partnership in AI-assisted programming into one of the clearest commercial consequences yet of the widening conflict between OpenAI and companies controlled by Elon Musk.

The important distinction is that OpenAI is ending a supply contract, not blocking the Cursor application outright. OpenAI's newly updated help documentation says Cursor users should still be able to use OpenAI models through their own API keys, through the Codex IDE extension, or through compatible third-party gateways. The exact date of the contractual cutoff is also not fully final: OpenAI describes November 12 as its proposed transition date and says Cursor could choose to end access earlier.

What OpenAI confirmed

OpenAI published its decision on August 28, saying it had notified SpaceX of its intention to wind down the agreement that provides OpenAI models inside Cursor. The company said it was giving the maximum notice allowed under the contract and that the relationship had lasted nearly four years.

According to OpenAI, the contract contains a limited cancellation window triggered by a change of control. Cursor announced on August 14 that it had officially been acquired by SpaceX, completing a process that followed an April partnership under which Cursor began using SpaceXAI infrastructure to expand model training.

OpenAI's stated rationale goes beyond ordinary vendor strategy. The company says it cannot be confident that SpaceX will use its technology within OpenAI's terms, citing what it describes as previous contract and terms-of-service violations by other Musk-controlled companies. Those allegations are OpenAI's position, not an independent legal finding about Cursor's conduct. OpenAI explicitly said it respects Cursor's team and product and acknowledged that developers are the people most directly affected by the transition.

November 12 is proposed, not yet immutable

OpenAI's help center gives the clearest practical wording for customers. It says the company is planning to wind down the Cursor contract and has proposed a transition period ending November 12. During that period, OpenAI says Cursor would continue receiving access to the models it uses today. The help page adds that Cursor may choose to end access sooner and that OpenAI will publish the official termination date once it is confirmed between the companies.

That nuance matters because headlines saying Cursor will simply “lose GPT” can overstate what has been announced. The supply route that powers Cursor's bundled OpenAI access is being targeted. Direct use of OpenAI services remains possible through other mechanisms.

How Cursor users can keep using OpenAI models

OpenAI lists three alternatives for developers who want to continue using its models in Cursor after the commercial agreement ends. The first is bring-your-own-key access, where a user connects an OpenAI API key to Cursor's local Chat and Agent features and is billed at OpenAI API rates.

The second route is the Codex IDE extension, which can run inside Cursor and use either a ChatGPT subscription or an OpenAI API key. The third is an AI gateway provider, where developers connect through a compatible service such as a cloud platform or another OpenAI-compatible gateway.

Those options mean the practical impact will differ by customer. Teams that depend on Cursor's bundled model selection, negotiated enterprise terms or centralized billing may face a more disruptive migration than individuals already using their own keys. Enterprise administrators will also have to review data handling, procurement and security policies before switching routes.

Why the SpaceX acquisition changed the relationship

Cursor's acquisition created the contractual opening OpenAI is now using. Cursor said on August 14 that joining SpaceX would give it access to what it described as the world's largest fleet of GPUs, with the goal of training stronger models at lower operating cost. The company had already partnered with SpaceXAI in April to use additional computing capacity for model training.

The strategic direction is increasingly clear. Cursor has been moving from a product that mainly brokers access to outside foundation models toward a company that also develops its own models. It highlighted Grok 4.6 shortly before the acquisition announcement as an example of what the combined infrastructure could produce.

That makes the OpenAI decision both a contractual dispute and a competitive reset. Cursor can rely more heavily on models built within the SpaceX ecosystem and other outside providers, while OpenAI is pushing Codex as its own direct developer product.

What remains uncertain

Several details are still unsettled. Cursor has not published a detailed migration plan explaining which OpenAI models will remain available under each subscription tier after the supply agreement ends. The companies have not announced a jointly confirmed final cutoff date. It is also not yet clear whether enterprise customers with separate arrangements will experience the same timeline.

Reuters reported that Cursor CEO Michael Truell said discussions with OpenAI were continuing. That leaves room for operational details to change even if OpenAI's broader decision to exit the supply agreement remains in place.

OpenAI also referenced its upcoming Astra model when explaining the decision, saying it did not want future models supplied under an arrangement where it lacked confidence in terms compliance. The company has not said that Astra will never be usable from Cursor through every possible route. Its statement is specifically about what it will provide under the existing commercial contract.

A bigger battle over who controls the coding stack

The dispute lands during a period when AI coding products are becoming strategically valuable interfaces rather than simple add-ons. The editor controls the developer workflow, the model provider controls core intelligence, cloud providers control compute, and increasingly the largest players want to own more than one layer.

Cursor's move into SpaceX gives it access to training infrastructure and in-house models. OpenAI is investing in Codex and direct IDE integrations. Anthropic has built a major developer business around Claude Code. The result is a market where model access, distribution and ownership are becoming tightly connected.

For users, this can produce both better competition and more fragmentation. An editor that supports multiple models can reduce dependence on one vendor, but contracts between the vendors can still determine what appears in a paid plan, how usage is priced and which new models arrive first.

What happens next

The next concrete date to watch is November 12, 2026, unless Cursor ends bundled OpenAI access sooner or the companies agree on a different final transition date. Before then, Cursor is likely to clarify its model lineup and migration options, while OpenAI says it will continue supporting developers who want to use its models through other channels.

The confirmed story is therefore narrower, and more consequential, than a simple product removal: OpenAI is invoking a change-of-control clause to end a long-running model-supply deal after SpaceX bought Cursor, reshaping one of the most visible partnerships in AI coding while leaving direct OpenAI access technically possible for developers who choose another route.
