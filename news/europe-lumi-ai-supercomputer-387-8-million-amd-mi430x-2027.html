Europe orders €387.8 million LUMI-AI supercomputer with AMD MI430X GPUs

EuroHPC has signed a procurement contract with France's Bull for a new AI-focused machine in Kajaani, Finland. The system is scheduled for the second half of 2027 and is expected to deliver ten times the AI capacity of the current LUMI supercomputer.

By VISIONE Wire · August 31, 2026 · 11:55 CEST

Europe has committed €387.8 million to a new AI-optimised supercomputer that is meant to turn public computing infrastructure into a larger strategic asset for researchers, startups and industry. The European High Performance Computing Joint Undertaking, or EuroHPC JU, said on August 31 that it signed a procurement contract with Bull for LUMI-AI, a next-generation system to be installed at CSC – IT Center for Science in Kajaani, Finland.

The machine is expected to become available in the second half of 2027. EuroHPC says LUMI-AI will use next-generation AMD Instinct MI430X GPUs together with 6th Gen AMD EPYC processors with 256 cores, and will serve as the computing backbone of the LUMI AI Factory. AMD and Bull say the new system is expected to provide roughly ten times the AI capacity and nearly twice the conventional high-performance computing capability of the current LUMI supercomputer.

What is confirmed: EuroHPC signed a €387.8 million acquisition, delivery, installation and maintenance contract with Bull. Funding is split 50-50 between EuroHPC and the six-country LUMI AI Factory consortium. Deployment is scheduled for the second half of 2027 in Kajaani, Finland. Performance claims such as “10x AI capacity” are vendor and project estimates for the planned system, not independent benchmark results from installed hardware.

What Europe is buying

LUMI-AI is not simply a refresh of the existing LUMI machine. It is being designed specifically around AI training, inference, large data sets and scientific workloads that increasingly overlap with machine learning. EuroHPC describes the system as able to support large-scale, dynamic and potentially confidential data while still handling traditional simulations and other data-intensive research.

According to EuroHPC and AMD, the compute layer will combine AMD Instinct MI430X accelerators with 6th Gen EPYC processors. Bull says its BullSequana XH3500 architecture will provide the underlying system platform. IBM is expected to supply storage based on IBM Storage Scale, while Nokia will contribute data-centre networking alongside Bull's own BXI interconnect.

The hardware mix matters because Europe's AI strategy is not only about model development. Training and serving advanced models requires tightly coupled accelerators, high-bandwidth networking, large storage systems and a software stack that can move data efficiently between them. In that sense, LUMI-AI is closer to a complete industrial-scale computing environment than to a single giant server.

A €387.8 million public investment

The total contract value covers acquisition, delivery, installation and maintenance. EuroHPC will provide half of the money through the Digital Europe Programme. The other half will come from the LUMI AI Factory consortium, which includes Finland, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Norway and Poland.

That funding model is important. Instead of each participating country building an isolated national machine, the countries pool resources into shared infrastructure while retaining access through agreed allocation mechanisms. EuroHPC also runs wider access calls that allow eligible European researchers, public bodies and companies to apply for computing time.

Reuters reported that the deal is Bull's largest contract to date. The company was separated from debt-laden Atos earlier in 2026 and is now under French state ownership, making the project both a computing purchase and a piece of Europe's wider industrial-policy puzzle.

Why LUMI-AI matters for Europe's AI race

The United States and China dominate the conversation around frontier AI because they combine deep capital markets, large cloud platforms, advanced chips and major AI laboratories. Europe has strong research institutions and industrial companies, but access to very large pools of AI compute has been a persistent strategic concern.

EuroHPC's AI Factories initiative is one response. The programme is building a network of AI-focused supercomputing centres intended to give startups, small and medium-sized companies, researchers and public institutions access to infrastructure that would otherwise be difficult to finance independently. EuroHPC said LUMI-AI is the sixth next-generation AI Factory procurement contract it has signed.

The promise is straightforward: lower the barrier to doing compute-intensive AI work in Europe and keep more sensitive research and industrial workloads inside European-controlled infrastructure. The harder question is whether access, software tooling, model expertise and commercial adoption will scale as quickly as the hardware.

Ten times the AI capacity, but the benchmark details still matter

Bull and AMD say LUMI-AI will provide a tenfold increase in AI capacity and almost double the HPC capability of the current LUMI system. Those are meaningful headline numbers, but they should be read as project expectations rather than completed benchmark results.

AI performance can be measured in many ways: peak floating-point throughput, training time for a specific model, inference latency, memory capacity, memory bandwidth, energy efficiency or performance under real multi-user workloads. The final practical advantage will depend on system configuration, software optimisation and how efficiently applications use the hardware once the machine is installed.

The current LUMI already relies heavily on AMD accelerators. Its GPU partition uses Instinct MI250X technology, which gives the project an existing software and operational base for AMD hardware. The move to MI430X is therefore both a major generational upgrade and a continuation of an established architecture family.

Energy and heat reuse are part of the design

Large AI systems consume enormous amounts of electricity, so energy design is not a decorative sustainability feature. Bull says LUMI-AI will use direct liquid cooling with warm water, allowing heat generated by the supercomputer to be recovered and reused in Kajaani's district-heating network.

The project also says the machine will run on 100% renewable electricity. The existing LUMI installation has already made heat reuse and low-carbon power central to its operating model, and LUMI-AI is designed to extend that approach at a much larger AI scale.

That does not make the environmental cost disappear. Manufacturing accelerators, building data centres and operating very large computing systems still carry material resource and energy demands. But heat recovery and low-carbon electricity can substantially change the operational footprint compared with a system powered by a more carbon-intensive grid and dumping its waste heat.

The quantum connection

LUMI-AI is also planned to integrate with LUMI-IQ, a quantum computer being added to the same broader environment. EuroHPC has been building a European quantum-computing infrastructure that links quantum systems with conventional supercomputers.

For most near-term users, the AI and HPC resources will remain more immediately useful than quantum computing. Still, colocating the systems gives researchers a test bed for hybrid workflows in which conventional processors, accelerators and quantum hardware can be combined for specialised problems.

What happens next

The contract announcement is the beginning of the deployment phase, not the arrival of a finished machine. Bull and its partners must manufacture, integrate, install and validate the system before users can rely on it. EuroHPC currently says LUMI-AI should be installed and available during 2027, while Bull and AMD specify the second half of the year.

Several details remain to be watched: the final accelerator count, memory configuration, measured AI and HPC performance, allocation rules for commercial users, software availability and the real-world power efficiency of the completed machine. Those figures will determine whether the system's promised tenfold AI gain translates into equally dramatic improvements for actual workloads.

For now, the clearest signal is strategic. Europe is choosing to spend hundreds of millions of euros on shared, publicly backed AI compute instead of leaving advanced model development entirely to hyperscale cloud providers. LUMI-AI will not close the transatlantic compute gap by itself, but it will give European researchers and companies a much larger machine on which to test whether sovereign AI infrastructure can become a practical competitive advantage rather than just a policy slogan.
