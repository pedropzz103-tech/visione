Shein shares fall 8% in Hong Kong debut as valuation drops to about $24 billion

The fast-fashion company opened its public-market chapter below the IPO price, underscoring investor concern about tariffs, regulatory pressure, profitability and the durability of its low-cost cross-border model.

By VISIONE Wire · Published August 31, 2026 · Updated September 1, 2026 at 07:55 CEST

Shein's long road to the public markets ended with a weak first trading session in Hong Kong. Shares of Shein Global Holdings fell about 8% in morning dealing on September 1, according to Reuters, trading around HK$44.6 after the company priced its initial public offering at HK$48.56 a share. At that level, the fast-fashion group was worth roughly $24 billion, below the approximately $26.5 billion valuation implied by the IPO price and dramatically below its nearly $100 billion private-market peak in 2022.

The move does not by itself determine how Shein will trade over the coming months. First-day price action can be noisy, especially when only a small portion of a company is freely available to trade. But the direction is notable because the IPO had already been priced at a steep discount to the company's former private valuation. Investors were therefore being offered Shein after a major reset, and the market still pushed the shares lower.

What changed since the original report: trading has now begun. HKEX confirmed that Shein's Class B shares, stock code 00625 and short name SHEIN-W, would start dealing on September 1. Reuters reported that the shares fell about 8% in morning trading to around HK$44.6, reducing the company's market value to roughly $24 billion.

The debut in numbers

Shein raised about $1.74 billion in the offering after selling shares at HK$48.56 each. The deal represented about 6.6% of the enlarged share capital, according to Reuters. Cornerstone investors took roughly one-fifth of the IPO allocation and are subject to six-month lockups, leaving only around 5% of the company freely tradable immediately after listing.

That limited free float matters when interpreting the first session. A relatively small pool of shares can amplify price movements, while allocations to long-term or locked investors reduce the amount available for ordinary trading. Even so, the weak opening gives the market's first real-time answer to a question that private funding rounds could not settle: what price are public investors willing to pay for Shein under today's trade and regulatory conditions?

The answer, at least in the first hours, was less than the IPO price.

Why investors remain cautious after a massive valuation reset

Shein's valuation has already undergone one of the sharpest repricings among major consumer technology and e-commerce companies of the past several years. The company was valued at about $98.2 billion in a private funding round in 2022, when pandemic-era e-commerce growth, cheap capital and enthusiasm for digitally native retail supported aggressive multiples.

At roughly $24 billion in early Hong Kong trading, Shein is worth only about one quarter of that peak. The fall is not simply a story about investor sentiment. Several pillars of the company's original economics have become more difficult.

The United States ended the de minimis duty exemption that had allowed many low-value cross-border parcels to enter without the same tariff burden applied to larger commercial shipments. Europe has also tightened rules and fees around low-value imports. Those changes directly affect a business model built around rapid, inexpensive shipments of low-priced goods from a highly responsive supplier network.

Reuters reported that Shein's net income fell 39% last year and that the company swung to a net loss in the first quarter. Shein has also indicated that first-half operating margins are likely to remain under pressure from higher customs duties, tariffs, fees and logistics costs in Europe and the Middle East.

The IPO was not met with AI-style demand

Hong Kong's IPO market has been exceptionally active in 2026, particularly around artificial intelligence, semiconductors and robotics. Some technology listings have attracted extraordinary retail demand. Shein's order book looked much more restrained by comparison.

Reuters reported that the retail portion of the offering was subscribed 5.63 times and the international tranche 2.59 times. Those are respectable numbers in a conventional IPO, but they are modest beside some recent Hong Kong technology offerings that have been oversubscribed by hundreds of times.

That contrast is useful. Shein arrives on the exchange as one of the world's best-known consumer internet brands, but investors are not valuing it as a hyper-growth technology platform. They are treating it more like a large global retailer with meaningful execution, regulatory and margin risk.

HKEX built a full trading ecosystem around the listing

The Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing group had prepared for the debut beyond the cash shares themselves. HKEX announced that weekly and monthly options on Shein would become available on September 1, subject to the shares listing as planned. The exchange also made the stock eligible for short selling and allowed issuers to list derivative warrants linked to Shein shares.

That matters because it gives professional investors tools to hedge or take bearish positions from the start. A mature derivatives ecosystem can improve liquidity over time, but it also means Shein enters public markets with far more mechanisms for investors to express skepticism than it ever faced as a private company.

What Shein says it will do next

At the Hong Kong opening ceremony, Chief Financial Officer Leigh Gui said the company would continue to innovate, optimize operations and work with supply-chain partners. Founder and CEO Sky Xu attended but did not make a public speech, according to Reuters.

The company has been trying to broaden its business beyond its own-label ultra-low-cost fashion. It has expanded its third-party marketplace, purchased U.S. apparel brand Everlane in May and said it wants to provide marketplace and supply-chain services to additional brands. Its prospectus also points to continued investment in technology and international growth.

That diversification is strategically important because the regulatory environment has made the pure cross-border fast-fashion model less forgiving. If tariffs and package fees permanently raise fulfillment costs in the United States and Europe, Shein needs either higher prices, better margins elsewhere, new geographic growth, more third-party marketplace revenue or a combination of all four.

What the first-day decline does and does not mean

An 8% fall is a meaningful signal, but it is not a verdict on the business. The most important public-market tests are still ahead.

Investors will now be able to evaluate Shein using recurring financial disclosures rather than private-company snapshots. Revenue growth, gross margin, operating profit, cash generation, customer acquisition costs and regional performance will matter much more than a single trading session. The market will also be watching whether tariff pressure stabilizes and whether the company can grow outside its most mature Western markets without sacrificing profitability.

One valuation comparison is already instructive. Reuters cited an estimate that Shein entered trading at roughly 15 times forward earnings, more than double the multiple of PDD Holdings, owner of Temu. That helps explain why some investors may still see the stock as expensive even after the collapse from its 2022 valuation. The question is not whether Shein is cheaper than it used to be. It is whether its expected earnings growth justifies a premium to listed peers facing many of the same trade and consumer pressures.

A public company at last, but under a harsher spotlight

Shein spent years attempting to list in New York and then London before Hong Kong became the viable route. Political scrutiny, supply-chain concerns and regulatory complexity repeatedly complicated those plans. The eventual listing therefore represents an operational milestone, but it also removes the shelter of private-market pricing.

From September 1 onward, every new tariff rule, earnings release, regulatory action and growth target can be translated almost instantly into a public share price.

The first translation was unfavorable: about 8% down and a valuation near $24 billion. The more consequential question is whether that becomes the beginning of a stabilization story after years of repricing, or whether public investors continue to demand an even larger discount for the risks embedded in Shein's global model.
