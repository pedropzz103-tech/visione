Fed's Warsh puts inflation first at Jackson Hole and says AI could reshape growth

The Federal Reserve chair used his first Jackson Hole keynote to say price pressures remain the central bank's dominant near-term problem, while laying out a quieter communications strategy and describing artificial intelligence as a possible new factor of production.

By VISIONE Wire · Published August 28, 2026 · 18:00 CEST

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish-leaning but deliberately noncommittal message at Jackson Hole on Friday: inflation is still too high, the Fed's 2% target is fixed, broad financial conditions do not look particularly restrictive, and policymakers must be prepared to act if underlying price pressures do not move toward target clearly and quickly enough.

Warsh stopped short of promising an interest-rate increase at the September meeting. That distinction matters. His speech was a statement of policy discipline rather than a calendar-based signal. But the economic assessment he presented gives little comfort to investors looking for near-term easing: the chair said the Fed's predominant focus should currently be on prices, described recent inflation improvement as insufficient to establish a better underlying trend, and said the central bank still has work to do unless inflation returns toward 2% at adequate speed.

The speech also went well beyond the next rate decision. Warsh used the annual symposium to argue that routine forward guidance has outlived much of its usefulness, to set out a more restrained philosophy of central-bank communication, and to put artificial intelligence directly into the Fed's long-run policy framework. In his view, AI may become not merely another technology sector but a new economic input capable of changing productivity, investment, labor demand and the way monetary policy itself is interpreted.

What Warsh said about inflation

The clearest near-term message came from the inflation data. Warsh said the 12-month change in the personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, stands at 3.7%. On a six-month basis, he cited a 4.1% pace. Both remain well above the Fed's 2% objective.

Warsh also rejected the idea that a few better summer readings are enough to declare victory. He said the latest PCE and consumer-price reports were better than expected but did not convince him that the underlying trend had materially improved. His preferred cross-check is breadth: 54% of the 199 components in the PCE basket have risen by more than 3% over the past year, compared with 32% on average in the two decades before the pandemic. Over the past six months, 49% of components have risen at an annualized rate above 3%.

That breadth is important because it suggests inflation is not confined to a tiny cluster of volatile categories. Commodity prices also bear watching, Warsh said, while medium-term inflation expectations remain broadly stable. His message was that anchored expectations are an asset the Fed must protect, not a reason to tolerate persistent overshooting.

He was equally explicit about the target itself. The 2% PCE inflation objective, Warsh said, is a firm and fixed target. He argued that inflation does not automatically return to target on its own and placed responsibility for price stability directly on the central bank.

No rate-hike promise, but no easing signal either

Warsh's conclusion was carefully constructed. He said he was committing to a discipline rather than to a specific decision. That means the speech should not be read as a formal pre-announcement of a September rate move. The Federal Open Market Committee still has data to absorb before its next meeting, and Warsh emphasized repeatedly that policy should respond to current, actionable information rather than stale forecasts or predetermined paths.

At the same time, his description of the economy makes a dovish pivot harder to infer. He said broad financial conditions are difficult to characterize as restrictive. Credit spreads are near historically low levels, corporate bond and leveraged-loan issuance has been strong, and bank lending standards for commercial and industrial loans are on the easier end of their historical range. Real consumer spending has risen more than 2% over four quarters, while private domestic final purchases have grown at nearly a 3% pace so far this year.

The labor market also did not sound like an economy demanding emergency support. Warsh cited a 4.1% unemployment rate, low claims and a labor market broadly consistent with full employment. That balance leaves inflation as the problem receiving more weight in his current assessment.

External coverage immediately interpreted the speech as increasing the possibility of future tightening. The Associated Press described Warsh as signaling that rate increases may be needed if inflation remains elevated. The key qualification remains that Warsh himself did not attach that view to a specific meeting or promise a particular path.

Warsh wants a quieter Fed

One of the most consequential institutional parts of the speech was Warsh's attack on routine forward guidance. The practice became a standard monetary-policy tool after the 2008 financial crisis, with officials using speeches, projections and carefully calibrated statements to shape expectations about future rates.

Warsh argued that this approach can become self-defeating in normal times. If markets rely heavily on the Fed's own guidance while the Fed relies on market prices as information, policymakers can end up observing reflections of their own messages rather than independent economic signals. He described that as a hall-of-mirrors problem.

His proposed alternative is not secrecy. It is less commitment to a mechanical future path and more emphasis on the principles governing decisions. Warsh said short-term interest rates should remain the predominant tool for the dual mandate and that unconventional crisis policies should generally be reserved for genuine emergencies. He also rejected the idea that a simple reaction function or Taylor-rule-style formula can substitute for judgment in a world shaped by changing technology, supply chains and geopolitics.

For markets, that points toward a Fed that may be harder to trade from prepared phrases. Warsh explicitly said central-bank communication should not encourage investors to look primarily to the Fed for their next trade. If that philosophy lasts, economic releases, Treasury-market signals, credit conditions, commodities and business activity could regain some influence relative to central-bank verbal choreography.

AI moves into the Fed's economic model

The most unusual section for a Jackson Hole monetary-policy speech concerned artificial intelligence. Warsh called AI a potentially new factor of production and said the Fed is studying how it may affect productivity, employment, capital intensity, market structure and even the equilibrium economics of model access.

He cited estimates putting annualized token sales at the two leading AI labs above $100 billion, up more than 500% from a year earlier. Those figures were presented as reported estimates rather than official Fed statistics, but Warsh's broader point was unmistakable: the scale of AI-related economic activity has become too large for monetary policymakers to treat it as a niche technology story.

Warsh also said more than half of this year's growth in business capital expenditure can likely be attributed to the AI buildout. Investment in equipment and intangible assets has been growing at roughly 9% over four quarters, the fastest pace since 2021. If AI produces sustained productivity gains, it could increase the economy's supply capacity. If it instead mainly creates an investment boom, labor disruption or concentrated returns to scarce assets, the policy implications would be different.

The Fed has created a task force on productivity and jobs to study those questions. Warsh stressed that its future recommendations will not determine current rate decisions. That separation is crucial: AI is part of the central bank's structural outlook, but it is not an excuse to override present inflation data.

Why this matters beyond Wall Street

The Jackson Hole speech ties together three forces that increasingly collide in the global economy. First, inflation remains above target despite years of tightening and periodic improvement. Second, financial conditions and private demand remain resilient enough that the Fed cannot easily argue policy is crushing activity. Third, enormous AI investment may be lifting productivity and corporate earnings while simultaneously changing the supply side of the economy in ways that traditional models are only beginning to capture.

That combination creates a harder policy problem than a simple recession-versus-inflation tradeoff. If AI investment raises productive capacity quickly, growth can run faster without generating as much inflation. If the productivity payoff arrives slowly while investment spending, energy demand and capital markets surge immediately, the transition can instead add near-term demand pressure. Warsh did not claim to know which path will dominate.

His answer was therefore methodological: rely less on promises about the future, inspect broad real-time evidence, treat 2% inflation as binding and preserve room to change course as the facts change.

What comes next

The next major test is the September 15–16 FOMC meeting. Between now and then, policymakers will receive additional inflation, labor-market and activity data. Markets will also watch Treasury yields, credit conditions and commodity prices, all of which Warsh explicitly named as useful signals.

The political backdrop will remain intense. President Donald Trump has called for lower borrowing costs, while Warsh's speech emphasized institutional responsibility for delivering stable prices. The chair did not make the address about politics, but his insistence on a fixed 2% target and on policy discipline places the Fed's inflation mandate at the center of any coming disagreement over rates.

Bottom line

Warsh's first Jackson Hole keynote as Fed chair did not deliver a rate decision in disguise. It delivered a framework. Inflation at 3.7% on the Fed's preferred 12-month measure is still too high, labor markets remain broadly healthy, financial conditions are not obviously restrictive and the central bank's near-term attention is therefore concentrated on prices.

At the same time, Warsh is trying to redesign how the Fed talks to markets and how it thinks about an economy being reshaped by AI. The immediate signal is cautious and inflation-focused. The larger signal is that this Fed wants fewer promises, more optionality and a policy model that treats artificial intelligence as a macroeconomic variable rather than merely a technology-sector story.
