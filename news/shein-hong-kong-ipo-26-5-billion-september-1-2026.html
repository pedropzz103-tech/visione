Shein's Hong Kong IPO values the company at about $26.5 billion ahead of September 1 debut

The fast-fashion group is set to raise roughly $1.7 billion at HK$48.56 a share. The listing completes a years-long route to public markets, but the valuation is more than 70% below Shein's 2022 private-market peak.

By VISIONE Wire · August 31, 2026 · Updated 08:00 CEST

Shein is approaching its first day as a publicly traded company with a very different price tag from the one attached to the fast-fashion phenomenon at the height of the pandemic-era e-commerce boom. The China-founded, Singapore-headquartered retailer is set to raise about $1.7 billion in Hong Kong after pricing its initial public offering at HK$48.56 a share, according to Reuters reporting based on people familiar with the transaction. That price implies a valuation of roughly $26.5 billion.

The shares are scheduled to begin trading on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange on September 1 under stock code 00625. The transaction brings to a close a prolonged attempt to reach public markets after Shein previously pursued listings in New York and London and encountered regulatory and political obstacles.

What is confirmed: Shein launched an offering of about 280 million shares in a marketed range of HK$47.60 to HK$49.50. Reuters reported that the deal was set to price at HK$48.56, raising about HK$13.6 billion, or $1.7 billion, and valuing the company near $26.5 billion. Regular exchange trading is scheduled for September 1.

The numbers behind the IPO

The scale of the reset is the defining number in this listing. Shein was valued at $98.2 billion in a private funding round in 2022. A valuation near $26.5 billion means public-market investors are assigning the company little more than one quarter of that peak value.

The global offering comprises approximately 280 million shares. The original marketed range of HK$47.60 to HK$49.50 would have raised as much as HK$13.86 billion, or about $1.77 billion, at the top. Reuters reported on August 27 that the final pricing was set near the midpoint at HK$48.56, for proceeds of roughly HK$13.6 billion.

The order book had already been fully covered earlier in the week, Reuters reported, with demand from existing shareholders as well as China-focused and multi-strategy funds. Cornerstone investors led by Boyu Capital, Tiger Global and General Atlantic committed about $383 million. Other disclosed participants include Tencent, Greenwoods, Taikang Life and UBS Asset Management.

Why Shein is worth far less than in 2022

A lower IPO valuation does not by itself mean the business has collapsed. It does mean the assumptions investors are willing to make about its future have changed substantially. Shein's earlier valuation reflected extraordinary e-commerce growth, abundant venture capital and expectations that its ultra-fast supply chain could keep expanding at exceptional rates.

Public investors are now looking at a more mature business facing tougher trade rules, higher compliance costs, intense competition and slower growth. The United States removed the de minimis import-duty exemption that had been important to low-value cross-border parcels. Europe has also increased scrutiny of low-cost e-commerce imports, while regulators in several jurisdictions have examined consumer protection, environmental claims and supply-chain practices.

Reuters reported earlier in August that Shein swung to a $99 million quarterly loss after the U.S. duty change and a $328 million fair-value charge related to convertible redeemable preferred shares following an accounting change. Those figures help explain why investors are no longer applying the kind of growth multiple the company commanded four years ago.

A listing after New York and London stalled

Shein's route to Hong Kong matters almost as much as the amount it is raising. The company spent years exploring a U.S. listing, then shifted attention to London. Both routes became entangled in scrutiny of the company's China-linked supply chain, labor practices, regulatory exposure and geopolitical sensitivities.

Hong Kong ultimately became the viable venue. The listing also arrives during a powerful revival in Chinese and Hong Kong equity issuance, especially around artificial intelligence, semiconductors and robotics. Associated Press reported that IPO proceeds in Hong Kong and Shanghai have already exceeded last year's totals, with more than $54 billion raised in 2026 and the two markets accounting for about 21% of global IPO volume.

Shein is an unusual passenger on that wave. It is not an AI or robotics company, and the sharp reduction in its valuation contrasts with the explosive first-day performances seen in some Chinese technology offerings. That makes its debut a useful test of investor appetite beyond the hottest technology themes.

Where the money is going

Shein has said that roughly 80% of the proceeds will be divided between technology investment and efforts to strengthen its brand and global presence. The prospectus allocation reported by multiple outlets puts about 40% toward technology capabilities and another 40% toward marketing and international expansion, with the remainder directed to corporate responsibility and general corporate purposes.

Technology spending is central to Shein's model. The company built its reputation around using demand signals and a tightly connected supplier network to place small initial orders, monitor consumer response and rapidly scale successful products. Investment in software, logistics, data systems and supplier coordination is therefore not merely an IT expense; it is part of the operating machinery behind the business.

What investors will watch on September 1

The first trading session will establish something Shein has never had before: a continuously visible public-market price. The HK$48.56 offer price is a transaction price for allocated IPO shares, not yet a price formed through normal exchange trading. Once trading begins, the market can push the shares above or below that level.

Three questions will matter immediately. First is whether the stock can hold its offer price after relatively restrained retail enthusiasm. Second is how investors weigh Shein's global scale against slowing growth and higher trade costs. Third is whether the broader enthusiasm surrounding Hong Kong listings is strong enough to support a consumer company whose valuation story is about compression rather than expansion.

A strong first day would not erase Shein's regulatory and profitability challenges. A weak first day would not automatically invalidate the business. IPO sessions can be distorted by allocations, limited initial float and short-term trading. The more consequential test will come over subsequent quarters as Shein reports as a public company and investors gain a clearer view of margins, cash generation and geographic performance.

The bigger signal from the $26.5 billion valuation

Shein's IPO is a reminder that becoming public changes the conversation. Private valuations can be established in relatively small funding rounds between a company and a limited group of investors. A major public listing asks a much larger market to price the business while absorbing real shares and comparing it continuously with listed competitors.

At nearly $100 billion in 2022, Shein was priced as an extraordinary growth story. At roughly $26.5 billion in 2026, the market is being asked to value a large global retailer that must defend its economics against tariffs, regulation, competition and a less forgiving capital environment.

That does not make the September 1 debut a simple referendum on fast fashion. It makes it a test of how much investors are willing to pay for scale when the easy-growth chapter has ended.
