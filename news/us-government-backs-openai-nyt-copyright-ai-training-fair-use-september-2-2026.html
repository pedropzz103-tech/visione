U.S. government backs OpenAI in New York Times copyright case

A federal filing puts Washington directly into one of the most important copyright fights of the generative-AI era, arguing that courts should not treat training large language models on copyrighted text as automatically unlawful.

By VISIONE Wire · September 2, 2026 · 19:58 CEST

The U.S. government has formally backed OpenAI in its copyright dispute with The New York Times and other publishers, arguing that the use of copyrighted text to train large language models can fall within fair use and warning that an overly restrictive interpretation of copyright law could damage American scientific, economic and national-security interests.

The intervention came through a statement of interest filed in federal court in Manhattan, where U.S. District Judge Sidney H. Stein is overseeing coordinated litigation against OpenAI and Microsoft. Reuters reported on September 2 that the filing appears to be the first time the federal government has taken a formal position in the current wave of lawsuits over the use of copyrighted works to train generative-AI systems.

What is confirmed: The United States filed a statement of interest in the coordinated OpenAI copyright litigation. The government argues that courts should reject a categorical theory that training LLMs on copyrighted text necessarily violates copyright law and says AI training is highly transformative. The filing does not decide the case, does not make every use of copyrighted material lawful, and does not eliminate the court's duty to apply the statutory fair-use factors to the facts before it.

What Washington is arguing

The government's position is broader than a defense of one company. According to the filing as reported by Reuters and legal publication MLex, the United States says it has a strong interest in preventing copyright doctrine from being interpreted in a way that unnecessarily constrains the development of large language models.

The filing emphasizes the transformative character of model training. In copyright law, transformation matters because one part of the fair-use analysis asks about the purpose and character of the challenged use. A use that serves a new function rather than merely substituting for the original work can weigh in favor of fair use, although it is never the only factor.

Washington also links the legal question to policy. The brief points to scientific research, economic growth and national security as reasons the United States has an interest in preserving room for AI development. That framing is consistent with the administration's March 2026 National AI Legislative Framework, which says creators and publishers should be protected while also stating that AI systems must be able to make lawful fair use of material they learn from.

The government is not the judge

A statement of interest can be influential, but it is not a ruling. The Justice Department is not joining the litigation as a plaintiff or defendant, and Judge Stein is not required to adopt the government's interpretation.

The distinction is especially important because fair use in the United States is fact-specific. Courts generally examine four statutory factors: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount and substantiality used, and the effect of the use on the potential market for the original.

That means the central legal question is more complicated than whether a training process is technically transformative. Publishers can argue that massive copying harms licensing markets or enables competing substitutes. AI developers can answer that models learn statistical relationships rather than functioning as searchable archives of the training corpus, and that training serves a different purpose from publishing the underlying articles.

What The New York Times alleges

The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023, alleging that millions of Times articles were copied without permission to develop products that can compete with the newspaper and, in some circumstances, reproduce or closely summarize protected material.

The litigation has since become a major test of how existing copyright law applies to generative AI. Other authors, publishers, music companies and rights holders have brought related cases against OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google and other technology companies.

OpenAI disputes the Times' characterization of its systems and has long argued that training qualifies as fair use. On its own case-information page, the company says the litigation has narrowed over time and that several claims have been dismissed, leaving fair use as a central issue. That page represents OpenAI's litigation position and should not be read as an independent account of disputed facts.

The administration had already signaled its view

The September filing is important partly because it converts a policy preference into a position taken inside an active federal court case.

In March, the White House's National AI Legislative Framework said the administration believes training AI models on copyrighted material does not itself violate copyright law, while also acknowledging that contrary arguments exist. Notably, the framework said Congress should allow courts to resolve whether particular training practices constitute fair use rather than legislating the answer in advance.

The new court filing follows that logic. It tells the judiciary what position the executive branch believes best fits copyright law and national policy, while leaving the actual judgment to the court.

The Copyright Office has taken a more qualified approach

The U.S. Copyright Office has separately studied generative-AI training. Its 2025 Part 3 report on copyright and AI examined the ingestion of copyrighted works, fair use, licensing and potential market effects. The Office did not reduce the problem to a universal rule that all AI training is lawful or unlawful. Its analysis stressed that outcomes can depend on the source of the material, the purpose of the use, the way models are deployed and the effect on existing or potential markets.

That makes the government's new filing especially consequential. It strengthens the pro-fair-use side of the policy debate, but it does not erase the fact-sensitive questions that courts still have to answer.

Why this case matters beyond The New York Times

The economic stakes extend far beyond one newsroom and one AI company. Frontier models require enormous datasets. If courts conclude that much of that training requires licenses, AI developers could face substantial new costs and a licensing market could become a standard part of model development. If courts instead hold that broad categories of training are fair use, rights holders may have less leverage to demand payment for ingestion of their works, though they could still pursue claims involving outputs, copying from unlawful sources or other conduct.

The ruling could also influence how publishers negotiate with AI companies. A growing number of media organizations have signed licensing or product partnerships with AI developers. Those deals coexist with litigation because commercial agreements can make sense even when the legal baseline remains uncertain.

What the filing does not settle

The government's intervention does not establish that OpenAI wins. It does not decide whether every dataset used by OpenAI was obtained lawfully, whether particular model outputs infringe copyrights, or whether alleged market substitution changes the fair-use balance.

It also does not bind judges in other AI copyright cases. Different cases involve different plaintiffs, datasets, model behavior and evidence. Courts can therefore reach different conclusions without necessarily contradicting one another.

That fragmentation is already part of the legal landscape. Reuters noted that early judicial decisions involving generative-AI training have not produced a single uniform rule. Appeals and later rulings are likely to shape the doctrine further.

What happens next

Judge Stein must still evaluate the parties' evidence and legal arguments. The government's filing becomes another piece of that record, potentially giving OpenAI and Microsoft a significant institutional ally on the core fair-use question.

For publishers and creators, the next phase will be watched for how much weight the court gives to claimed licensing markets and competitive harm. For AI companies, the crucial issue will be whether the court accepts the idea that training is sufficiently transformative to carry substantial weight even when entire copyrighted works are copied during the process.

For policymakers, the case is now a bridge between two arenas that had largely run in parallel: Washington's effort to maintain U.S. leadership in AI and the judiciary's attempt to apply decades-old copyright doctrine to model training at unprecedented scale.

Bottom line

The September 2 development does not end the AI copyright fight. It does, however, change its political and legal context. The U.S. government is no longer merely saying in policy documents that fair use should leave room for AI training. It has taken that position directly into one of the cases that could define the rules for the entire industry.
