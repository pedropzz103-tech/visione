Nvidia Q2 revenue hits $96.2 billion as data center sales double

Nvidia beat its own $91 billion revenue guide, posted $89.0 billion in Data Center sales and pointed to a $108 billion third quarter as the AI infrastructure boom keeps accelerating.

By VISIONE Wire · Published August 22, 2026 · Updated August 27, 2026 · 08:12 CEST

Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter revenue of $96.2 billion for the three months ended July 26, 2026, up 18% from the previous quarter and 106% from a year earlier. The result came in above the company's own midpoint guidance of $91 billion and reinforces the scale of the global spending wave around artificial-intelligence infrastructure.

Data Center remained the engine of the company. Revenue from that segment reached $89.0 billion, up 18% quarter over quarter and 117% year over year. Put differently, more than nine dollars of every ten Nvidia generated in the quarter came from the infrastructure business serving cloud providers, model developers, enterprises and large-scale AI projects.

GAAP gross margin was 75.0%. GAAP operating income reached $63.7 billion, up 124% from a year earlier, while GAAP net income was $59.7 billion, up 126%. Diluted GAAP earnings per share were $2.46. On a non-GAAP basis, Nvidia reported diluted earnings per share of $2.22.

The result cleared an already extreme bar

The comparison point matters because Nvidia entered the quarter with expectations that would have looked implausible for a semiconductor company only a few years ago. Fiscal first-quarter revenue had already reached $81.6 billion. Management then guided to roughly $91 billion for Q2, plus or minus 2%, while assuming no Data Center compute revenue from China.

The actual $96.2 billion figure therefore exceeded the midpoint by about $5.2 billion. It also means Nvidia added roughly $14.6 billion of quarterly revenue in just three months. The company is now operating at a scale where a single quarter of incremental revenue can be larger than the annual sales of many public chipmakers.

The most important number remains Data Center. At $89.0 billion, that business is now so dominant that Nvidia's financial profile is effectively tied to continued deployment of AI systems by hyperscalers, frontier-model labs, governments and enterprises. Gaming and other businesses still matter strategically, but they no longer define the company's growth story.

Nvidia guides to $108 billion next quarter

Nvidia expects fiscal third-quarter revenue of $108.0 billion, plus or minus 2%. It also expects GAAP and non-GAAP gross margins of 74.0%, plus or minus 50 basis points, with operating expenses of roughly $9.2 billion on a GAAP basis and $9.0 billion non-GAAP.

The China assumption remains unusually important. Nvidia explicitly says its Q3 outlook does not assume any Data Center compute revenue from China. That caveat means the $108 billion midpoint is built without a market that historically represented a meaningful source of accelerator demand.

For investors, the implication is two-sided. On one hand, Nvidia is forecasting another large sequential increase even without China Data Center compute sales. On the other, export restrictions and policy shifts still represent a material uncertainty because access to China could change both revenue opportunity and competitive dynamics over time.

Vera Rubin moves into full production

Alongside the financial results, Nvidia said its Vera Rubin platform is ramping into full production, with systems running at partners including CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Oracle Cloud Infrastructure and Nebius. The company is also expanding the broader rack-scale ecosystem around networking, CPUs, storage processing and security.

This product transition is central to the next phase of the story. Blackwell established Nvidia's current generation of large-scale AI systems, while Vera Rubin is positioned as the next platform for training, inference and increasingly agentic workloads. Nvidia's ability to move customers between architectures without a long revenue pause is one of the main reasons investors watch production comments almost as closely as quarterly sales.

Founder and CEO Jensen Huang framed the quarter around AI moving from experimentation toward productive economic use. That is management's argument, not an independently verified market-wide conclusion, but the financial numbers show that customers are still committing extraordinary sums to compute infrastructure.

AI infrastructure is becoming a financing story too

Nvidia also highlighted partnerships intended to support more than $500 billion of third-party capital for AI infrastructure over time, subject to definitive agreements. The counterparties named by Nvidia include Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR.

That detail matters because the AI buildout is no longer only a technology spending cycle. It is increasingly a capital-markets cycle involving debt, project finance, private credit, power contracts, real estate and long-duration infrastructure commitments. As projects become larger, the cost and availability of capital can become as important as chip supply.

The risk is not that strong Nvidia revenue automatically proves every AI data-center project will earn an attractive return. Revenue confirms demand for Nvidia's products. It does not by itself establish the profitability of every customer deployment, nor does it settle the debate over how quickly AI services will monetize the infrastructure being installed.

Profitability remains unusually high

Nvidia's 75.0% GAAP gross margin is notable because the company is scaling increasingly complex systems rather than selling only individual chips. High margins at this stage suggest that demand, product mix and pricing power remain strong enough to absorb the costs of rapid platform transitions.

GAAP operating income of $63.7 billion was 19% higher than the prior quarter and 124% above the same period last year. Net income rose more slowly sequentially, up 2% from Q1, but remained 126% higher year over year.

The company returned about $26 billion to shareholders during the quarter through share repurchases and cash dividends. Nvidia said approximately $99 billion remained under its share-repurchase authorization at the end of Q2. Its next quarterly dividend is $0.25 per share, payable October 1 to shareholders of record on September 10.

What is confirmed and what remains uncertain

Confirmed: fiscal Q2 revenue was $96.2 billion; Data Center revenue was $89.0 billion; GAAP gross margin was 75.0%; GAAP net income was $59.7 billion; and Nvidia's Q3 revenue outlook is $108 billion, plus or minus 2%.

Confirmed by company guidance: Nvidia is not assuming any China Data Center compute revenue in the Q3 outlook, and Vera Rubin is ramping into full production with multiple major cloud partners.

Still uncertain: the pace at which customer AI spending converts into durable profits, how export policy will evolve, whether infrastructure financing remains cheap enough to support the largest projects, and how much competitive pressure eventually comes from custom accelerators or rival GPU platforms.

Company claim requiring outside validation: Nvidia's broader framing that AI has reached an economic inflection point. The quarter clearly demonstrates exceptional demand for compute, but the return on that compute will vary by customer and application.

Why the result matters beyond Nvidia

Nvidia has become a read-through for a much wider industrial chain. Strong Data Center sales can support demand expectations for advanced memory, networking, server manufacturing, cooling, power equipment, data-center construction and cloud services. A weaker outlook would have sent the opposite signal.

The $108 billion Q3 guide therefore matters not only to Nvidia shareholders. It suggests that, at least from Nvidia's order book and current visibility, AI infrastructure spending is still expanding rather than plateauing. That can influence expectations across technology stocks, utilities, data-center operators and credit markets.

At the same time, scale raises the burden of proof. When quarterly revenue is already near $100 billion, maintaining triple-digit year-over-year growth becomes mathematically harder. Investors will increasingly focus on how fast the next generation of systems ramps, whether margins hold and whether customers keep raising capital expenditures at the same pace.

What happens next

The next major checkpoint is Nvidia's fiscal third quarter. The market will compare actual revenue against the $108 billion midpoint and watch whether Data Center growth continues to outpace the rest of the company. China policy will remain a standing variable, and Vera Rubin deployment will become a more concrete test of Nvidia's ability to extend its current lead into another product cycle.

Customer earnings will matter too. Cloud providers and major technology companies are committing large sums to AI capacity, so their own results will provide evidence about utilization, monetization and the financial returns behind the spending Nvidia is recording as revenue.

Bottom line

Nvidia's Q2 numbers did more than beat its previous guidance. They showed that the AI infrastructure buildout is still accelerating at an extraordinary scale: $96.2 billion in quarterly revenue, $89.0 billion from Data Center and a $108 billion midpoint for the next quarter.

The unresolved question is no longer whether customers are spending. They are. The harder question is how durable that spending becomes once the industry moves from building capacity to proving economic returns. For now, Nvidia's results show that the construction phase is still running at full speed.
