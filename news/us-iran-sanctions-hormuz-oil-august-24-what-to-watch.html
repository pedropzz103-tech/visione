US-Iran sanctions showdown puts Hormuz and oil back at center of markets

Washington is preparing another round of economic pressure on Iran for Monday, August 24, while tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains severely constrained and the risk of retaliation is keeping energy markets on edge.

By VISIONE Wire · August 22, 2026 · 18:00 CEST

The United States and Iran entered the weekend trading increasingly hostile messages ahead of a planned U.S. sanctions announcement on Monday, August 24, adding a fresh layer of risk to an already disrupted global energy market.

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said details of the new measures will be released Monday. President Donald Trump has warned of economic consequences for countries that provide what he described as a lifeline to Tehran. Iran, meanwhile, has rejected the pressure campaign and warned that further threats could trigger a severe response.

The immediate significance is not simply diplomatic. The confrontation is unfolding around the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world's most important energy chokepoints, where shipping remains dramatically below pre-war levels. Before U.S.-Israeli attacks on Iran began on February 28, roughly one-fifth of global oil and liquefied-natural-gas supplies passed through the strait, according to Reuters.

What is confirmed

Several pieces of the story are established. Trump said on August 21 that he did not believe Tehran was ready to make what he called the "right deal." Bessent has said the administration intends to outline new sanctions on Monday. The White House already has a legal framework allowing additional tariffs on goods from countries that directly or indirectly purchase Iranian goods or services.

That February executive order does not automatically mean every Iranian trading partner will face the same tariff. It provides a mechanism under which the Commerce Department can determine whether a country is acquiring Iranian goods or services and the State Department can then recommend whether and to what extent additional U.S. duties should be imposed.

That distinction matters. The specific countries, entities, financial channels or tariff rates covered by Monday's announcement were not yet confirmed as of Saturday afternoon. Any claim that a particular country will definitely be hit before the Treasury publishes the measures would be speculation.

Hormuz remains the pressure point

The Strait of Hormuz has become the physical center of the confrontation because disruptions there can transmit geopolitical risk directly into global energy prices. Reuters-linked shipping data showed just seven commodity vessels transited the strait on Thursday, August 20, about half the previous day's tally and far below normal pre-war activity.

Iran still retains missile and drone capabilities capable of threatening commercial traffic. U.S. sanctions rules also create another layer of complexity: the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control says U.S. persons are not authorized to make payments to the Iranian government or the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps for safe passage through Hormuz, nor may they receive related services from those entities.

That combination means shipping companies are not dealing with a normal congestion problem. They are balancing physical security, sanctions compliance, insurance costs, routing constraints and the possibility that rules governing permitted passage could shift again.

Iraq receives limited passage

One notable development on August 22 was Iran's decision to grant permission for a number of Iraqi oil tankers to pass through Hormuz after repeated requests from Baghdad, according to Iran's state news agency IRNA as reported by Reuters.

The permission does not amount to a general reopening of the strait. Traffic remains sharply reduced and vessels still face security risks. But the Iraqi arrangement shows that passage can be negotiated selectively, creating a system in which political relationships and case-by-case permissions increasingly shape energy flows.

Iraq is particularly exposed. It produced roughly 4 million barrels of oil per day before the conflict, while Baghdad has been exploring alternative export routes through Turkey, Syria and Jordan to reduce reliance on Hormuz.

Oil is already pricing the risk

Energy markets moved higher into the weekend. Brent crude settled Friday at $94.39 a barrel, up 0.65% on the day, while U.S. West Texas Intermediate finished at $87.06, up 0.26%, according to Reuters market reporting. Brent gained 6.39% over the week and WTI rose 5.66%.

Those moves do not prove that traders expect a new military escalation. Oil prices are responding to several forces at once, including constrained Middle East supply, reduced Iranian exports, alternative shipping routes, U.S. production and changing expectations about how aggressively Washington will enforce sanctions.

Still, the direction of travel is clear: the more difficult it becomes to move barrels through or around Hormuz, the larger the risk premium embedded in crude prices.

Why China matters

China is likely to be one of the most closely watched countries when the U.S. measures are announced because it is a major buyer of Iranian energy. Reuters reported that Washington's threatened penalties could affect Iran's most important trading partners, including China.

The February White House order is broad enough to cover countries that directly or indirectly purchase Iranian goods or services. But the operational details matter more than the headline authority. Enforcement could range from designations against specific companies and financial intermediaries to broader tariff measures affecting trade with the United States.

Until Treasury and other agencies publish the actual package, the scale of exposure for Chinese buyers and other trading partners remains uncertain.

The sanctions architecture was already tightening

Monday's expected announcement would build on an existing pressure campaign rather than start one from scratch. OFAC's Iran sanctions page lists multiple Iran-related actions during July and August. Treasury has also targeted Iranian exchange houses, shipping networks and procurement channels connected to the government and the IRGC.

In July, Treasury sanctioned a network associated with Mohammad Hossein Shamkhani, describing it as an illicit shipping and sanctions-evasion operation tied to Iranian oil exports and global commodities trading. Other July actions targeted weapons-procurement networks and financial facilitators.

OFAC has separately warned maritime stakeholders about sanctions risks connected to demands for payments or guarantees for Hormuz passage. That guidance underscores how the financial and maritime dimensions of the confrontation are now intertwined.

What is political rhetoric and what is policy

Trump's public statements and Iran's warnings are politically significant, but they are not the same thing as a published sanctions instrument. The measures that matter operationally will be the Treasury designations, licenses, executive actions, tariff notices or regulatory guidance released by U.S. agencies.

Likewise, Iranian statements about retaliation do not establish that an attack will occur. The relevant factual indicators are actual military movements, verified shipping incidents, formal restrictions on navigation and confirmed changes to energy exports.

That separation is especially important in a fast-moving conflict where statements can be designed to deter an adversary, reassure domestic audiences or influence negotiations.

What happens next

The first major checkpoint is Monday, August 24, when Bessent has said the United States will provide details of the new sanctions. Markets will look for three things: who is targeted, whether secondary measures reach major foreign buyers of Iranian energy and how quickly enforcement begins.

Shipping data through Hormuz will be the second checkpoint. A sustained increase in transits would suggest that workarounds, permissions or security arrangements are easing the bottleneck. A renewed decline, particularly if accompanied by attacks, would put upward pressure on risk premiums.

The third checkpoint is diplomacy. As of August 22, the two sides were not reported to be actively pursuing a new peace agreement. Trump said Tehran was not ready for the deal Washington wants, while Iranian officials continued to reject U.S. pressure.

The result is a fragile equilibrium: no confirmed return to full-scale exchanges of fire at the moment, but no durable settlement either. Sanctions are now being used as the next lever in that contest, with Hormuz ensuring that the consequences extend far beyond Washington and Tehran.
