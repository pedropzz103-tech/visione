U.S. pushes G20 toward lighter AI regulation with Carolina Principles

Washington is using a G20 technology meeting in North Carolina to argue that governments should avoid broad new AI-specific rules and regulators unless genuinely new risks demand them. The proposal arrives just weeks after the European Union moved into active enforcement of major parts of its AI Act.

By VISIONE Wire · September 1, 2026 · 19:57 CEST

The United States is pressing G20 governments to take a lighter-touch approach to artificial intelligence regulation, using a two-day technology meeting in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, to promote a proposed framework called the Carolina Principles. The core argument is that governments should not automatically build new AI-specific regulatory systems every time the technology creates a new application or business model.

Reuters reported on September 1 that White House science and technology adviser Michael Kratsios is urging participating governments to reserve new regulation for genuinely novel considerations, expand investment in foundational research and create more commercial opportunities for emerging technologies. The administration also opposes creating new international bodies dedicated specifically to governing AI.

What is confirmed: The Carolina Principles are a U.S.-backed policy proposal being promoted at a G20 technology gathering in North Carolina. Reuters reports that the framework favors limited new AI-specific rulemaking, stronger research investment and greater commercial deployment. It is not yet a binding G20 agreement, and VISIONE Wire has found no final communique showing that all G20 members have accepted the framework.

What the Carolina Principles are trying to change

The proposal targets one of the central arguments in global technology policy: whether artificial intelligence should be governed through broad horizontal laws written specifically for AI or mainly through existing sector regulators and legal frameworks.

The U.S. position, as described in prepared remarks reported by Reuters, is that a new technology does not always require a new bureaucracy. A medical AI system, for example, can still fall under health and medical-device rules; an AI product operating in financial markets may already face securities, banking or consumer-protection obligations. The administration's preference is to add new requirements only when existing law cannot address a genuinely new problem.

That is a policy position, not a settled factual conclusion about which regulatory model produces the best outcomes. Supporters argue that sector-specific enforcement can adapt faster and avoid duplicative compliance costs. Critics argue that general-purpose AI creates risks that cross traditional regulatory boundaries and therefore can require common transparency, safety and accountability rules.

The timing puts Washington and Brussels in sharp contrast

The North Carolina meeting comes at a particularly clear moment in the transatlantic policy divide. The European Commission says the EU AI Act became broadly applicable on August 2, 2026, with the AI Office and national authorities beginning enforcement of major provisions.

Europe's framework includes transparency requirements for certain AI systems and generated content, governance rules for general-purpose AI models and a risk-based system for higher-risk applications. Some high-risk obligations have later effective dates, including rules that the Commission says will apply from December 2027 and, for AI embedded in regulated products, August 2028.

The contrast should not be reduced to “regulation versus no regulation.” The U.S. already has federal and state laws that apply to fraud, discrimination, privacy, intellectual property, national security and product safety, and the Trump administration published a national AI legislative framework in March. That White House framework itself calls for protections involving children, scams, intellectual property, energy costs and national-security risks while also emphasizing innovation and a uniform national policy.

The real disagreement is therefore about regulatory architecture and intensity: when a new AI-specific obligation is justified, who should enforce it and whether global coordination should create new institutions or work through existing ones.

Why the G20 venue matters

The G20 brings together the world's largest advanced and emerging economies, meaning its technology discussions can influence standards well beyond the countries that ultimately sign a particular communique. Even non-binding principles can shape national legislation, procurement rules, international standards work and the expectations companies face when entering new markets.

Reuters reports that the North Carolina gathering includes government ministers and senior technology executives. That matters because the companies building frontier AI systems have a direct commercial interest in whether compliance requirements remain fragmented across markets or converge around common principles.

The U.S. proposal also arrives amid intensifying competition with China. Open-weight Chinese AI models have become an increasingly important part of the global ecosystem, creating pressure on Washington to think about regulation not only as a safety question but also as a competitiveness and national-security issue. A rule that slows deployment may be viewed differently by policymakers if they believe rival technology ecosystems are advancing faster.

What the U.S. has already said at home

The Carolina Principles fit the administration's broader domestic policy. In March, the White House called on Congress to create a national AI framework that would prevent a patchwork of conflicting state laws, accelerate deployment and expand testing environments while retaining targeted protections.

That framework also called for data-center growth without shifting electricity costs onto households, stronger tools against AI-enabled scams, protections for children, respect for intellectual property and workforce development. In other words, the administration's international message is not that AI should operate outside law. It is that regulation should be targeted and should not create what officials view as unnecessary barriers to deployment.

What remains uncertain

The most important unresolved question is whether other G20 members will endorse the Carolina Principles in recognizable form. Countries enter the meeting with very different domestic systems. European Union members are already implementing the AI Act. Other governments are building their own mixes of voluntary standards, safety institutes, sector rules and national AI laws.

There is also no guarantee that agreement on high-level language would produce regulatory convergence. Terms such as “novel considerations,” “proportionate regulation” and “innovation-friendly” can mean very different things once lawmakers translate them into statutory requirements.

A second uncertainty is enforcement. A framework that discourages new AI agencies can still produce strict rules if existing regulators interpret their mandates aggressively. Conversely, a dedicated AI regulator does not necessarily mean every use of AI will be tightly controlled. Institutional design and actual enforcement matter as much as the headline philosophy.

What to watch next

The immediate test is the language that emerges from the North Carolina meeting. Any joint statement will show whether the U.S. has persuaded partners to adopt its preference for limited new AI-specific regulation, or whether the final text reflects a compromise with governments that favor stronger horizontal safeguards.

The larger test comes later in the year. The technology meeting feeds into a broader G20 process ahead of the leaders' summit in Miami in December. If the Carolina Principles survive into leaders-level language, they could become a significant reference point for governments deciding whether to build new AI institutions or lean on sector-specific law.

Europe's enforcement experience will also matter. The AI Act is now moving from legislative design into practical supervision, giving both supporters and critics real evidence about compliance costs, transparency and market effects. That evidence will inevitably shape the global argument over whether broad AI laws accelerate trust or slow innovation.

Bottom line

The Carolina Principles mark a clear attempt by Washington to export its preferred AI-governance model: regulate concrete harms, use existing institutions where possible, invest heavily in research and deployment, and avoid creating new AI-specific rules simply because the technology is new.

For now, that remains a proposal rather than a global settlement. The G20 meeting will show how much support the United States can build for it at a moment when the European Union is enforcing a far more explicit AI rulebook and governments worldwide are deciding which model, or combination of models, they want to follow.
