Google gets option for $12.2 billion Marvell stake in sweeping AI chip deal

A new custom-silicon agreement could generate enormous business for Marvell while giving Google another route to scale AI infrastructure beyond its traditional chip partnerships.

By VISIONE Wire · August 19, 2026 · 18:50 CEST

Google has dramatically widened its relationship with Marvell Technology in a custom-chip agreement that gives the search and cloud giant the right to buy nearly 59 million Marvell shares, a position worth roughly $12.2 billion if fully exercised at the agreed price.

The agreement centers on specialized hardware for Google's artificial-intelligence infrastructure. According to Reuters, Marvell will work on technology used around Google's tensor processing units, or TPUs, including processors for AI inference, storage components, networking controllers and near-memory computing. The warrant covers up to 58.97 million shares at an exercise price of $206.58 each.

The market reaction was immediate. Marvell shares jumped while Broadcom, Google's long-standing custom-chip partner, fell, a sign that investors see the agreement as potentially reshaping how Google spreads its AI semiconductor spending across suppliers.

Why the deal matters beyond Marvell

For years, the AI infrastructure race has been dominated by the cost and availability of Nvidia GPUs. Hyperscalers have responded by designing more of their own silicon, attempting to tune chips for their workloads and gain tighter control over both performance and economics.

Google is one of the most advanced players in that strategy. Its TPU family is used to train and run AI systems, and the company has increasingly positioned the hardware as a cloud product for external customers rather than merely an internal Google technology.

The Marvell agreement suggests that Google's custom-silicon push is expanding into a broader supply network. That does not necessarily mean Broadcom is being pushed out. Analysts cited by Reuters described the move as diversification in a growing market rather than a simple replacement of one supplier with another.

A giant incentive tied to giant spending

The warrant is particularly notable because it aligns Marvell's potential ownership structure with Google's future purchasing activity. Reuters reported that the arrangement could translate into roughly $120 billion in revenue for Marvell through fiscal 2033 if Google reaches the performance and purchasing thresholds attached to the option.

That figure is not guaranteed revenue. It depends on future targets being met. But its scale illustrates how much money is now moving through the physical layer of the AI boom: accelerators, memory, networking, storage and data-center infrastructure.

The Financial Times also reported that the collaboration includes specialized hardware connected to Google's TPU ecosystem and noted that the agreement comes as Google pushes TPUs more aggressively toward outside customers.

The bigger AI-chip chessboard

Marvell is not entering the race from nowhere. The company already supplies custom silicon and data-center technologies to large cloud operators, while Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom and a growing group of specialized chipmakers are competing for slices of a rapidly expanding AI infrastructure market.

Google's move therefore has two layers. The first is technical: securing more capacity and expertise for custom AI systems. The second is strategic: reducing dependence on any single supplier at a moment when compute has become one of the most important constraints in artificial intelligence.

The transaction also deepens a trend in which major AI companies and their infrastructure suppliers become financially intertwined. Instead of straightforward customer-vendor contracts, the industry is increasingly using warrants, investments and long-term purchasing commitments to lock in access to expensive hardware.

For Marvell, the deal is a major validation of its custom-silicon business. For Google, it is another move toward owning more of the AI stack beneath its models and cloud services. And for the wider chip market, it is a reminder that the battle around AI is no longer only about who builds the best model. It is increasingly about who controls the machines underneath it.
