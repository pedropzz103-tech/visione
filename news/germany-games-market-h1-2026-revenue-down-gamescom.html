Germany games market falls 3% to €4.2 billion in first half of 2026

Europe's largest games market is entering gamescom week with softer first-half revenue, a reminder that record event scale and enormous audience reach are not the same thing as uninterrupted market growth.

By VISIONE Wire · August 23, 2026 · 20:00 CEST

Germany's market for video games, games hardware and online gaming services generated €4.2 billion in the first half of 2026, a decline of 3% from the same period a year earlier, according to figures released by game, the German Games Industry Association, ahead of gamescom 2026.

The number arrives at an unusually revealing moment. Cologne is about to host the world's largest games event by physical scale, with more than 1,600 exhibitors from 67 countries and a record 233,000 square metres of gross exhibition space. Yet the domestic market surrounding that spectacle has slowed after a stronger 2025.

That contrast matters. It shows an industry that remains culturally enormous, globally connected and strategically important while still dealing with the less glamorous realities of spending cycles, hardware transitions, subscription economics and uneven consumer demand.

What the €4.2 billion figure tells us

The first-half estimate covers spending on games, online gaming services and games hardware in Germany. The association says the data is based on market research from YouGov Shopper and Sensor Tower. Compared with the first six months of 2025, total revenue was down 3%.

That does not erase the broader expansion seen last year. The association's 2026 annual report says the German games market generated around €9.4 billion in 2025, up 4% from 2024. Germany is described by the industry body as Europe's largest games market and the fifth largest worldwide.

The full-year 2025 breakdown also showed how uneven growth can be inside the same market. Revenue from games plus in-game and in-app purchases was about €4.9 billion, down 1%. Games hardware and accessories reached roughly €3.4 billion, up 12%, while online gaming services crossed €1 billion for the first time, rising 7%.

That pattern helps explain why a modest decline in early 2026 should not be read as a simple collapse in player interest. Hardware, software, services and recurring digital spending move on different calendars. A major console launch, a blockbuster release or a strong subscription cycle can materially change the mix from one half-year to the next.

Germany remains a huge audience

The scale of the player base is one reason Germany remains central to the European games business. According to the German Games Industry Association's annual report, 41.2 million people in the country play computer or video games. Among people aged 6 to 69, roughly six in ten play.

The average player is 38.3 years old, and 79% of players are adults. The over-60 population is the largest single age group in the association's breakdown, accounting for 18% of players. Women represent 46% of the player base and men 54%.

Smartphones are the most widely used gaming platform with 23.7 million users, followed by consoles with 22.1 million. Around 14 million people play on PC and 9.9 million on tablets. About 19 million players use two or more devices.

Those figures make the market structurally broader than the image of a niche enthusiast hobby. Games in Germany now sit across age groups, devices and business models, which also makes headline revenue more sensitive to changes in consumer spending across several categories at once.

Indie games are not a side story

The association's pre-gamescom figures also highlight the size of the indie audience. About 17 million gamers in Germany play indie games, according to the August 18 release.

That is especially relevant to gamescom 2026, where the indie area has expanded again. The event says its indie segment is larger this year while the overall show has reached more than 1,600 exhibitors. Organisers report that 70% of exhibitors come from outside Germany, with 47 country pavilions representing 40 countries.

In other words, the event is becoming more international and physically larger even as the domestic market posts a softer six-month revenue number. For smaller studios, gamescom is therefore not merely a reflection of German consumer spending. It is a global discovery and business platform located inside Germany.

The political argument is getting louder

The industry is also using gamescom week to push a policy case. In an August statement, the German Games Industry Association argued that games should be treated as part of Europe's technological and digital-sovereignty strategy rather than simply as entertainment.

Its survey data says six in ten people in Germany believe games help drive technologies of the future. Another 57% say policymakers have underestimated the potential of games for civic education.

The association has called for stronger conditions for games production in Germany and Europe, linking the sector to innovation, education and international competitiveness. That argument will have a prominent platform this week: German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier is due to become the first German head of state to attend gamescom, and the gamescom congress is scheduled to bring together political, industry and civil-society speakers.

Record gamescom scale does not guarantee record consumer spending

Gamescom itself enters 2026 in record territory. Organisers say the available exhibition area is fully booked for the first time, with 233,000 square metres of gross space. Saturday tickets and wildcards have sold out, and the event includes companies ranging from platform holders and major publishers to independent studios and technology providers.

Those numbers measure industry participation and demand for the event, not the same thing as retail market revenue. A trade and fan show can expand while consumer spending cools, particularly when exhibitors are global and the domestic revenue number covers only Germany.

That distinction is important for reading the 3% decline correctly. The data confirms a softer start to 2026, but it does not show a retreat from gaming as a mass medium. Germany still has more than 40 million players, a €9.4 billion recent full-year market and one of the most internationally significant events in the industry.

What to watch next

The next question is whether the first-half slowdown persists through the holiday period. The second half of a games year can be reshaped by major releases, hardware promotions and subscription growth, while gamescom itself often serves as the launchpad for marketing campaigns that feed into autumn demand.

There is also a broader industry test. If hardware and service growth continue to offset weaker spending on games and in-game purchases, Germany may end 2026 with a healthier mix than the headline first-half decline suggests. If weakness spreads across categories, the 3% drop will look more like the beginning of a broader slowdown.

For now, the confirmed picture is mixed rather than catastrophic: €4.2 billion in first-half spending, down 3%, set against a massive player base and a gamescom that is breaking records for exhibitors and floor space. The industry arrives in Cologne with plenty of attention, but also with a very real economic question hanging over the show floor.
