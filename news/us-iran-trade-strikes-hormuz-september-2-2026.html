U.S. and Iran trade strikes as Hormuz tensions flare again

A new exchange of U.S. and Iranian attacks has pushed the Gulf back toward open confrontation, with military bases, commercial shipping and the Strait of Hormuz once again at the center of the crisis.

By VISIONE Wire · September 2, 2026 · 17:58 CEST

The United States and Iran have entered another dangerous round of direct military exchanges, less than two months after a previous burst of fighting around the Strait of Hormuz. U.S. Central Command said American forces completed strikes against Iranian military targets on September 1, hitting Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps air-defense sites, radar systems, maritime assets, mine-laying capabilities and communications facilities. Iran responded overnight with missile and drone attacks aimed at U.S. positions and countries hosting American forces.

The immediate facts are still developing, and claims from the two sides do not fully align. CENTCOM says its strikes followed recent attempted attacks by the IRGC against commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and against U.S. service members. Iranian military statements, meanwhile, describe Tehran's attacks as retaliation for what it calls renewed U.S. aggression.

What is confirmed: CENTCOM publicly acknowledged a new wave of U.S. strikes inside Iran on September 1. The U.S. military says the targets included air defense, radar, maritime, mine-laying and communications capabilities. Iran subsequently launched missiles and drones toward U.S.-linked targets in the region. Some casualty and damage claims remain disputed or independently unverified.

What the United States says it struck

In its September 1 public release, CENTCOM said U.S. forces struck IRGC military infrastructure after attempted attacks on commercial vessels and American personnel. The command said more than 50,000 U.S. service members were operating across the Middle East and remained prepared for additional missions ordered by the president.

The target list matters because it points to the core of the current dispute: control of the maritime environment around the Strait of Hormuz. Air-defense and radar systems affect Iran's ability to detect and contest aircraft. Maritime facilities and mine-laying equipment directly affect shipping. Communications infrastructure supports coordination between those systems.

CENTCOM also released 58 seconds of official strike footage through DVIDS. The video is identified as U.S. Central Command material, dated September 1, and listed by DVIDS as public-domain U.S. military content subject to its standard distribution restrictions.

Iran answered with missiles and drones

Iranian military authorities said they began retaliatory attacks after the U.S. strikes. Reports citing official or semi-official Iranian statements said ballistic missiles and drones were launched toward U.S. assets and bases in several countries, including Bahrain, Jordan and other parts of the Gulf region.

Jordan's armed forces said air-defense systems intercepted 10 of 13 ballistic missiles that entered Jordanian airspace, with the remaining three falling in remote areas and no casualties reported. Bahrain's military also said it had intercepted Iranian aerial attacks. Those statements provide independent confirmation that the Iranian response extended beyond rhetoric and involved weapons crossing regional airspace.

Iran also claimed that it shot down a U.S. MQ-9 Reaper drone and inflicted losses on American forces. VISIONE Wire is not treating those claims as established facts because the available U.S. official material reviewed for this report does not confirm them.

Civilian casualty claims remain contested

Iranian media and officials have reported civilian deaths and injuries from the U.S. strikes, including at a wedding in Sirik County in Hormozgan province. Reports carried by Xinhua cited Iranian sources saying at least four people, including a child and a woman, were killed there and more than 50 were wounded. Later Iranian reporting described additional deaths elsewhere.

CENTCOM has not publicly confirmed those civilian casualty figures in the release announcing the operation. Because the battlefield information environment is highly contested and independent access is limited, the exact civilian toll should be treated as unresolved until additional evidence is available.

This distinction is important. A government statement can establish that a government is making a claim; it does not by itself establish that the underlying event occurred exactly as described. The same standard applies to American and Iranian assertions.

Hormuz is the pressure point again

The Strait of Hormuz remains the most consequential geography in the conflict. It is one of the world's most important energy chokepoints, connecting the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Any sustained interruption to tanker traffic can ripple quickly into crude prices, shipping insurance, freight rates and inflation expectations.

Reuters reported on September 2 that only four commodity vessels transited the strait on Tuesday, down from 10 the previous day and below the recent 10-day average. Oil prices initially climbed to their highest level in more than a month as traders assessed the renewed fighting, before retreating later in the session as the market weighed actual supply disruption against the risk of further escalation.

Gulf stock markets also fell following the exchange. That market reaction does not prove that a major supply shock is imminent, but it shows how quickly military events around Hormuz transmit into asset prices.

A conflict with a recent history

This is not the first exchange around the strait in 2026. In July, CENTCOM said it conducted three nights of strikes against more than 300 Iranian targets after attacks on commercial vessels. The U.S. military said those operations included missile and drone sites, naval capabilities, ammunition storage and coastal surveillance infrastructure.

That history makes the September fighting more dangerous. Each new round begins from a higher baseline of deployed forces, damaged infrastructure and political mistrust. Military actions that one side describes as deterrence can be interpreted by the other as preparation for a wider campaign.

What happens next

The first question is whether the September 1-2 exchange ends as a limited retaliation cycle or becomes the opening of another sustained campaign. CENTCOM's wording leaves open the possibility of additional operations, while Iranian military statements have threatened further strikes.

The second question is shipping. Vessel traffic through Hormuz, insurance costs and any new incidents involving tankers will provide some of the clearest signals of whether the crisis is spilling into the global economy.

The third question is casualties. Confirmed deaths among U.S. forces, Iranian forces or civilians could sharply narrow the political space for de-escalation. At the time of publication, several casualty claims remain unresolved.

Finally, regional governments are under pressure to defend their airspace while avoiding being pulled deeper into a U.S.-Iran confrontation. Missile interceptions over countries that host American forces create a widening geographic footprint even when those governments are not seeking direct participation in the conflict.

Bottom line

The new exchange is significant because it combines three risks at once: direct U.S.-Iran military confrontation, attacks reaching multiple Gulf states, and renewed pressure on the Strait of Hormuz. The verified facts show that both sides have already moved beyond threats. What remains uncertain is whether they now stop, or whether the region is entering another extended cycle of strikes and retaliation.
