Dell raises FY2027 outlook as AI server backlog reaches $95 billion

Dell Technologies reported another record quarter as demand for AI infrastructure accelerated sharply. The company booked $60.9 billion of AI server orders in three months, ended the quarter with a $95 billion AI backlog and lifted its full-year revenue forecast to $192 billion.

By VISIONE Wire · September 2, 2026 · 08:00 CEST

Dell Technologies has raised its fiscal 2027 outlook after reporting record second-quarter revenue of $47 billion and a surge in orders for AI infrastructure. The company says it booked $60.9 billion of AI server orders during the quarter, recognized $16.4 billion in AI server revenue and finished the period with a record $95 billion AI backlog.

The numbers place Dell among the clearest corporate beneficiaries of the global data-center buildout. They also show how quickly the scale of AI infrastructure spending is changing. Dell now expects full-year revenue of about $192 billion, up from its previous outlook of $167 billion, and expects roughly $74 billion in fiscal-year revenue from AI-optimized servers, compared with a prior projection of $60 billion.

What is confirmed: Dell reported fiscal Q2 2027 revenue of $47 billion, up 58% year over year. It recorded $60.9 billion in AI server orders, $16.4 billion in AI server revenue and a $95 billion ending AI backlog. The company raised its full-year revenue outlook to $192 billion and its non-GAAP diluted EPS outlook to $25.50. Reuters reported that Dell shares rose about 7% in extended trading after the results.

Record revenue, with AI doing much of the heavy lifting

Dell's fiscal second quarter was unusually large even by the standards of the current AI infrastructure cycle. Revenue increased 58% year over year to $47 billion. The company's Infrastructure Solutions Group, which includes servers, networking and storage, generated a record $31.8 billion in revenue, up 89% from a year earlier.

Within that division, AI infrastructure was the headline. Dell said it received $60.9 billion in AI orders during the quarter, its highest quarterly total ever. It recognized $16.4 billion of AI server revenue and ended the quarter with $95 billion in backlog, meaning booked demand remained far ahead of what had already been shipped and recognized as revenue.

Chief operating officer Jeff Clarke said on Dell's earnings call that the company has booked more than $130 billion of AI server orders over the past 12 months. He also said Dell's AI customer count has surpassed 6,500, with demand spreading across so-called neocloud providers, sovereign computing projects and traditional enterprise customers.

Reuters reported that the company's AI-optimized servers use Nvidia chips and are being bought by customers including cloud infrastructure providers Nscale and CoreWeave. That does not mean every dollar of Dell's backlog will convert into revenue on the same timetable, but the backlog provides a concrete measure of the demand already contracted or ordered.

Dell lifts its full-year forecasts again

The scale of the quarter forced a substantial reset of Dell's annual guidance. The company now expects fiscal 2027 revenue of about $192 billion, an increase of $25 billion from its earlier $167 billion outlook. It also raised its non-GAAP diluted earnings-per-share forecast to $25.50 from $17.90.

For AI-optimized servers specifically, Dell now expects fiscal 2027 revenue of approximately $74 billion, up from its earlier $60 billion forecast. The change is significant because Dell had already raised AI expectations earlier in the year. The latest revision suggests that orders are arriving faster than management expected even a few months ago.

Reuters noted that Dell's $47 billion quarterly revenue exceeded the $44.92 billion average analyst estimate compiled by LSEG. Adjusted earnings of $7.04 per share also came in above the $4.91 estimate cited by Reuters.

The boom is spreading beyond GPU servers

The quarter was not simply a story about racks filled with accelerators. Dell said revenue from traditional servers and networking rose 122% year over year. Management attributed part of that growth to ordinary data-center refresh cycles, but also to increased CPU demand associated with AI and agentic computing workloads.

That distinction matters. Training and inference clusters rely heavily on GPUs and other accelerators, but large AI deployments also require conventional compute, networking, storage, orchestration and security infrastructure. If AI spending continues to pull adjacent infrastructure categories upward, the commercial effect can extend well beyond the most visible accelerator suppliers.

Storage revenue increased 26% to $4.9 billion, while Dell said demand improved across PowerFlex, PowerStore, PowerProtect and PowerVault. The company also reported that its client business grew 20%, with commercial PC revenue up 22% and consumer revenue up 7%.

Clarke told investors that client revenue is growing at its fastest rate in five years. Reuters also reported that Dell has been raising product prices, including PC prices, to protect margins from an industry memory-chip shortage. That means part of the revenue acceleration reflects pricing as well as higher unit and infrastructure demand.

Margins improved despite the infrastructure surge

A persistent question around AI hardware is whether enormous revenue growth translates into equally attractive profit growth. In Dell's quarter, profitability improved materially. Gross margin dollars rose 78% to $9.9 billion, while operating income increased 160% to $5.9 billion.

Infrastructure Solutions Group operating income reached a record $4.8 billion, up 225%, with a 15% operating margin. Dell attributed the improvement to greater scale, pricing discipline and a richer mix of infrastructure revenue.

That is important because high-end AI systems can be expensive to assemble and deploy. Dell argues that its value is not limited to reselling components: large projects can require dozens of specialized designs covering power, cooling, networking and data-center configuration. The company says this engineering and deployment complexity helps differentiate it from simpler hardware distribution.

What the backlog says, and what it does not

A $95 billion AI backlog is a striking number, but it should not be treated as the same thing as $95 billion of completed sales. Backlog represents orders not yet recognized as revenue. Delivery timing, supply availability, customer schedules and contract changes can affect how quickly those orders become reported revenue.

Still, the backlog matters because it gives visibility into demand beyond the quarter that has already closed. Dell said its pipeline remains several times larger than its backlog even after converting more than $131 billion into AI orders during the past 12 months.

The inference is that the AI infrastructure cycle remains supply- and execution-constrained rather than demand-constrained for Dell at this stage. That is an interpretation, not a guarantee. A slowdown in hyperscale capital spending, changes in accelerator roadmaps, customer financing pressure or a broader economic downturn could alter that picture.

Why investors reacted

Dell shares rose about 7% in extended trading after the report, according to Reuters. The reaction reflects more than a simple earnings beat. Investors were given a higher annual revenue target, a higher profit target, a larger AI-server forecast and evidence that the company's backlog expanded dramatically even as shipments accelerated.

The results also arrive in a market that has repeatedly questioned whether spending on AI infrastructure can continue at its current pace. Dell's order book does not settle that debate, but it provides fresh evidence that corporate, sovereign and cloud customers are still committing very large sums to compute infrastructure.

What comes next

The next test is execution. Dell has to convert an unusually large backlog into systems that can be delivered, installed and recognized as revenue while maintaining margins and navigating constraints in accelerators, memory, networking gear and power infrastructure.

Investors will also be watching whether the demand mix continues to broaden beyond a relatively small group of large AI infrastructure buyers. Management says it is already seeing that expansion across enterprises and sovereign customers. Sustained diversification would make the current cycle less dependent on a handful of neocloud and hyperscale projects.

For now, the confirmed picture is straightforward: Dell's AI business is growing faster than the company expected, traditional infrastructure is rising alongside it, and management has again rewritten its fiscal-year targets upward. The $95 billion backlog is the clearest sign that the AI hardware buildout is not only a chip story. It is increasingly a full-stack infrastructure story as well.
